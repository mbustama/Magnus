\title{\magnus: neutrino oscillation probabilities for any Hamiltonian,\\
any number of flavors, any density profile}

\author{Mauricio Bustamante\corref{author}}
\ead{mbustamante@nbi.ku.dk}
\cortext[author]{ORCID: \href{http://orcid.org/0000-0001-6923-0865}{0000-0001-6923-0865}.}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}

A neutrino created with one flavor can \textit{oscillate} and be detected later with another. Where the matter it crosses has constant density, the probability of this happening is a closed form. Where the density varies continuously---inside the Sun, through a supernova envelope, along a chord through the Earth---it is not, and the usual recourse is to replace the profile by a staircase of constant steps, or to integrate the evolution equation numerically. We take a third route. \magnus\ propagates the evolution operator with the \textit{Magnus expansion}: over each segment of the trajectory the operator is written as a single exponential whose argument is a truncated series of time-ordered integrals of the Hamiltonian, evaluated by Gauss--Legendre quadrature. Every truncation of that series remains in the Lie algebra, so the operator is unitary \emph{by construction} rather than to the accuracy of the truncation, at any order and any setting. The decisive practical consequence is that a segment is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many oscillation cycles it spans: what sets its width is how fast the \emph{profile} changes, not how fast the \emph{phase} winds. A differential-equation solver has the opposite constraint, and pays for every radian. Nothing in the method fixes the size of the Hamiltonian or what it contains, so vacuum, matter, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation, sterile states, five flavors, combinations of these, and a Hamiltonian nobody has written down yet are one and the same call. \magnus\ also returns the phase-averaged, decohered probability---the quantity a solar experiment measures---in closed form, without propagating the $10^{4}$ radians that separate production from detection. We set out the method, the code, and a measured comparison against two other public codes, and we state where each of them wins.

\end{abstract}

Computation of neutrino flavor oscillation probabilities for an arbitrary number of flavors, in media whose density varies continuously along the trajectory, and for Hamiltonians that are time- or position-dependent --- including scenarios for which no closed form exists. \\ \\

{\em Solution method:}\\
The evolution operator is propagated with the Magnus expansion. Truncated time-ordered integrals of the Hamiltonian are evaluated by Gauss--Legendre quadrature over a chain of position slabs and exponentiated; every truncation remains in the Lie algebra, so the operator is unitary by construction. Slab widths are chosen adaptively from a requested tolerance, and slab edges are placed on the discontinuities of a layered profile. Where the accumulated phase is extreme, an adiabatic transport is composed with exact local Magnus patches across the non-adiabatic windows. The phase-averaged, decohered limit is available in closed form, without propagating. \\ \\
\smallskip
%%%%
{\em Additional comments, including restrictions, unusual features:}\\
The Hamiltonian must be Hermitian and the evolution unitary: decay to invisible states, absorption, decoherence as an open-system process, and collective (self-interaction) effects lie outside the method and need a density-matrix or nonlinear solver instead. The requested tolerances {\tt rtol} and {\tt atol} are a \emph{stopping criterion, not an error bound}: refinement halts when two successive levels agree (evidence of convergence and not a certificate of it). \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Neutrinos are created and detected in weak interactions as flavor states ($\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, $\nu_\tau$) but they propagate as superpositions of propagation states\ \cite{Pontecorvo:1967fh, Maki:1962mu, Bilenky:2004sn, Fantini:2018itu}. Because the superposition evolves, a neutrino created with one flavor has a non-zero probability of being detected with another. The observation of that in solar, atmospheric, reactor, and accelerator neutrinos led to the discovery of neutrino mass and of flavor mixing in leptons\ \cite{Kajita:2016cak, McDonald:2016ixn}.

Computing the flavor-transition probability means solving the Schr\"{o}dinger equation
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:schrodinger}
 i\,\frac{d}{dl}\,\nu_\alpha(l) = \mathbb{H}(l)\,\nu_\alpha(l) \;,
\end{equation}
where $\nu_\alpha$ represents the flavor state of a neutrino born with flavor $\alpha = e$, $\mu$, $\tau$, $\mathbb{H}$ is the Hamiltonian in flavor space, and $l$ is the distance traveled by the neutrino since its creation along the trajectory, which for relativistic neutrinos---the predominant scenario---stands in for the elapsed time (\ie, $l \approx t)$. The probability of a $\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta$ transition is, therefore, $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \beta}(l) = \vert \nu_\beta^\dagger \nu_\alpha(l) \vert^2$.  Through $\mathbb{H}$, the probability is dependent on the neutrino energy. The probabilities are oscillatory in $l$; hence, flavor transitions are conventionally referred to as ``neutrino oscillations''

The cost of computing flavor-transition probabilities reflects the complexity of $\mathbb{H}$, itself an encoding of the environment in which the neutrino propagates and the physics it is subject to. Where $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on $l$, the solution of \equ{schrodinger} is simply $\nu_\alpha(l) = e^{-i \mathbb{H} l}\,\nu_\alpha(0)$---with $\mathbb{U} \equiv e^{-i \mathbb{H} l}$ the evolution operator---and the problem is closed: the oscillation probabilities in vacuum and in matter of constant density have exact solutions. The companion code {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} evaluates them for an arbitrary Hamiltonian at two, three, and four flavors. This covers important scenarios such as the propagation of astrophysical neutrinos in the vacuum of outer space and of natural and human-made neutrinos in the Earth's crust.

One quantity governs everything that follows. The \emph{accumulated phase} along a trajectory is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:phase}
 \Phi
 \equiv
 \int_{l_0}^{l_1} \left[\lambda_{\rm max}(l) - \lambda_{\rm min}(l)\right] dl \;,
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\rm max}$ and $\lambda_{\rm min}$ the largest and smallest instantaneous eigenvalues of $\mathbb{H}(l)$. It is the total phase difference the fastest-separating pair of propagation states builds up between production and detection, measured in radians. It is what any method that follows the oscillation must resolve. In vacuum at three flavors it reduces, \eg, to the familiar $\Delta m^2_{31} L / 2E$, where $\Delta m^2_{31} \equiv m_3^2 - m_1^2$ is the difference in the masses of neutrino mass eigenstates $\nu_3$ and $\nu_1$ (which are the propagation states in this case), $L$ is the total path length, and $E$ is the neutrino energy. For a 1-GeV neutrino crossing the Earth, $\Phi$ is of order 10 rad; for a 5-MeV neutrino leaving the Sun, some $1.3 \cdot 10^{4}$ rad; and for a TeV neutrino from $100$~Mpc, some $10^{15}$ rad.

The difficulty this paper addresses is where the Hamiltonian varies \emph{continuously} along the path. The more common manifestation of this is the propagation of neutrinos in matter of continuously varying density. Then $\mathbb{H}(l)$ does not commute with itself at different positions and the evolution operator is not simply the exponential of the Hamiltonian. This leaves two standard recourses. One is to replace the matter profile by a staircase: cut the path into slabs of constant density, solve each in closed form, and compose the operators in order. The other is to integrate \equ{schrodinger} numerically with a differential-equation solver. Each has a characteristic failure. The staircase converges only as fast as a piecewise-constant approximation to a smooth function (second order in the slab width). An adaptive solver must instead resolve the oscillation phase step by step, so its cost grows with $\Phi$. Neither is about the physics in the Hamiltonian; both are about the shape of the profile and the size of $\Phi$.

\section{Synopsis}
\label{sec:synopsis}

To address this, we introduce \magnus\ \cite{Magnus}, a code that returns oscillation probabilities by computing the evolution operator using the \textit{Magnus expansion}\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz, Blanes:2008xlr}. Over a segment of the trajectory of the neutrino---within which the Hamiltonian may be varying---the expansion allows the evolution operator to be written as a single exponential, $\mathbb{U} = \exp(\Omega)$, where $\Omega$ is a series of 
% time-ordered 
integrals of nested commutators of the Hamiltonian. 

What that form buys is three advantages, independently of what the Hamiltonian contains: robustness, speed, and flexibility. \magnus\ is \emph{robust}: because the evolution operator is exactly unitary at any order, any tolerance, and any slab count, the probabilities always add up to one, within numerical round-off. \magnus~is \emph{fast where the phase is large}: cost follows the profile and not the phase, where a differential-equation solver pays for every radian. And \magnus~is \emph{flexible}: the Hamiltonian is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix of any size, so five flavors, non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, and a completely novel neutrino interaction are one and the same call. Later, we illustrate and quantify the above properties.

Moreover, \magnus~does not rely solely on the Magnus expansion: it seamlessly hands over the neutrino evolution to adiabatic solvers when they offer an accuracy or speed advantage over the Magnus expansion. This allows \magnus~to overcome situations where the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonians are close to each other, without the need for dedicated user intervention.

Finally, \magnus~ships with Hamiltonians that tackle commonly explored scenarios---including propagation in vacuum, in uniform matter, inside the Earth and the Sun, through a supernova shock---and using both standard and new-physics cases. The latter includes, but is not limited to, non-standard neutrino-matter interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation, sterile neutrinos, and pseudo-Dirac neutrinos. \magnus~ships these cases readily available for up to five neutrinos flavors (\ie, three active flavors, plus two additional ones), but is easily extendable to more. Further, \magnus~also returns the \emph{phase-averaged} (decohered) form of the probabilities, \ie, the $L/E \to \infty$ limit a solar or astrophysical measurement is sensitive to because it integrates over many cycles of a phase that cannot be resolved. Finally, \magnus ~ships optimized functions to compute and render commonly used plots in the field, such as of probability vs.~distance and vs.~energy, oscillograms, and bi-probability plots.  The application programming interface (API) is extensively documented and made publicly available, along with implementation details, and a large number of tutorial notebooks.

% We present the method in full later. For now, two properties follow.

% First, \textbf{truncating the series preserves total probability (\ie, the sum of all probabilities is unity) even if it yields an approximate answer}. Each term of $\Omega$ is a real combination of nested commutators of anti-Hermitian matrices and is therefore itself anti-Hermitian, so any truncation stays in the Lie algebra and its exponential is \emph{exactly} unitary. Probabilities are non-negative and their rows sum to one (to numerical round-off at implementation) at any truncation order, any quadrature accuracy, and any slab count. What that buys is not exactness but independence: the residual defect does not respond to the accuracy dials at all, growing only with the number of factors composed. Figure~\ref{fig:validation} shows this beside the accuracy. The unitarity defect and the accuracy live at different scales; the former does not move even when the latter does.

% Second, \textbf{a segment is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many oscillation cycles it spans}. When $\mathbb{H}$ does not vary, every nested commutator vanishes, the series terminates at its first term, and $\exp(-i\mathbb{H}\Delta l)$ is not an approximation to be refined but the answer. What forces a segment to be narrow is therefore the variation of the \emph{profile} across it, not the phase accumulated inside it. A differential-equation solver has the opposite constraint. Section~\ref{sec:phase_vs_profile} measures both statements.

% Three further properties follow from the same construction and are not shared by the closed forms. There is no ceiling on the number of flavors. The SU($n$) constructions that give closed-form probabilities need the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian in radicals, which the Abel-Ruffini theorem forbids past four~\cite{Bustamante:2019ggq}. 
% There is no ceiling on the number of flavors. Nothing in the Magnus expansion refers to the size of the matrix at all; we ship two, three, four, and five flavors with the code, but there is no ceiling on what the Hamiltonian contains. In the code, it is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix, so non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, sterile states (or all of them at once), or a completely novel term are the same call with a different matrix. In addition, \texttt{Magnus} also returns the \emph{phase-averaged} (decohered) limit of the probabilities, \ie, the $L/E \to \infty$ limit a solar or astrophysical measurement returns because it integrates over many cycles of a phase that cannot be resolved, computed in closed form from the eigenvectors of $\mathbb{H}$ rather than by propagating that phase.

% The Magnus expansion is due to Magnus\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz} and has a large literature in numerical analysis, where it is one of the standard geometric integrators---methods that preserve a structure of the problem exactly rather than to within the truncation error\ \cite{Iserles:2000acta, Hairer:2006gni}. \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr} reviews it, and \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit} derives the Gauss--Legendre \emph{collocation} integrators that \magnus\ uses by default---collocation meaning that the Hamiltonian is sampled at a few chosen points inside each step, here the Gauss--Legendre nodes, and the scheme built so that those samples deliver the full order.

% The same expansion is known in magnetic resonance as average Hamiltonian theory, where it arrived in 1968\ \cite{Waugh:1968, Haeberlen:1968zz, Evans:1968} and became a design discipline rather than only a series: pulse sequences are chosen so that named orders of the expansion vanish identically, with symmetry arguments canceling whole classes of higher-order terms\ \cite{Haeberlen:1976}. What we provide is its implementation for neutrino flavor evolution: the expansion carried to order 10, the quadratures, the slab chain and its adaptive refinement, an exactly unitary matrix exponential, the composition with adiabatic transport for extreme accumulated phase, the closed-form decohered limit, and the engineering that makes a whole scan over energy or arrival direction one batched call rather than thousands of separate ones.

% The expansion has been used for neutrino oscillations before. It was brought to two-flavor evolution in matter by D'Olivo and Oteo\ \cite{DOlivo:1990xs}, developed for realistic profiles by Ioannisian and Smirnov\ \cite{Ioannisian:2008ve}, and turned into a practical integrator for the three-flavor problem by Casas \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Casas:2016asi}, who report gains of up to two orders of magnitude in speed over general-purpose differential-equation solvers. What has not been available is a general-purpose, tested, documented implementation. It takes an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at an arbitrary number of flavors, chooses its own discretization from a requested tolerance, and places its slab edges on the discontinuities of a layered profile. It also says which of its six internal engines answered a call---the independent methods that can serve a request, listed in Section \ref{sec:engines}---and reports, rather than assuming, when the evidence for its own convergence is weak, naming how far from converged it stopped.

% Two limits bound everything that follows. The Hamiltonian must be Hermitian, which is what makes the evolution unitary and the system closed; and the Magnus series is guaranteed to converge absolutely while $\int \lVert \mathbb{H} \rVert_2\, dl < \pi$ over the interval the expansion is applied to\ \cite{Moan:2008conv}. Here $\lVert \cdot \rVert_2$ is the spectral norm, the largest singular value, which for a Hermitian matrix is the largest eigenvalue in modulus. The condition is sufficient rather than necessary---a particular $\mathbb{H}$ may converge well beyond it---but it is sharp in the sense that a counterexample exists at $\pi$, so it is the bound to design slabs against. The first is a restriction on the physics and Section \ref{sec:limitations} says what it excludes. The second is not, because the interval is the code's to choose: a long trajectory is cut into as many slabs as the criterion needs. \magnus\ warns when a slab looks unsafe, testing $\lVert \Omega \rVert_2 \ge \pi$ rather than the integral itself. Since $\lVert \Omega \rVert_2 \le \int \lVert \mathbb{H} \rVert_2\,dl$, that is a lower bound on the quantity in the criterion. The test therefore under-reports. A slab it complains about is genuinely outside the bound, so there are no false alarms; a slab it passes may still be outside, so silence is not a guarantee. It is cheap, and it is the conservative half of the check rather than the whole of it.

\section{Neutrino oscillations: notation and conventions}
\label{sec:recap}

In a system of $n \geq 2$ neutrino flavors, $\nu_\alpha$ is the flavor state of a neutrino, represented by an $n$-component column vector, and $\mathbb{H}$ is the $n \times n$ Hamiltonian in flavor space. We use natural units, $c = \hbar = 1$, with energies in eV and distances in eV$^{-1}$, so that $\mathbb{H}L$ is dimensionless. \textbf{Every length crossing the package's interface is in eV$^{-1}$}: baselines, radii, slab edges, breakpoints alike. The factor {\tt globaldefs.UNIT\_KM} converts them into kilometers, one kilometer being some $5.07 \cdot 10^{9}$~eV$^{-1}$. This is the one convention worth stating as a rule rather than meeting case by case, because getting it wrong does not raise errors: a baseline handed over in kilometers is a baseline a few meters long, and the call returns a converged, unitary probability for it. \magnus\ warns when a length is small enough to be kilometers left unconverted, but does not halt.

Because neutrinos are relativistic, we identify the elapsed time with the distance traveled and write both as $l$. The state obeys \equ{schrodinger}; its solution defines the evolution operator $\mathbb{U}(l_1, l_0)$  through $\nu_\alpha(l_1) = \mathbb{U}(l_1,l_0)\, \nu_
\alpha(l_0)$, with $\mathbb{U}(l_0,l_0) = \mathbb{1}$. The probability that a neutrino born as $\nu_\alpha$ is detected as $\nu_\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:probability}
 P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}(l_1, l_0)
 =
 \left\lvert \left[\mathbb{U}(l_1,l_0)\right]_{\beta\alpha} \right\rvert^2 \;.
\end{equation}
We take $\mathbb{H}$ Hermitian throughout; $\mathbb{U}$ is then unitary and $\sum_\beta P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} = 1$ for every $\alpha$. Flavor indices run over $e, \mu, \tau$ at $n=3$ and over $e, \mu, \tau, s_1$ at $n = 4$ and $e, \mu, \tau, s_1, s_2$ at $n = 5$, the states $s_i$ being sterile, which interact only through their mixing.

As an example, in vacuum, the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h_vacuum}
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm vac}(E)
 =
 \frac{1}{2E}\, \mathbb{R}\, \mathbb{M}^2\, \mathbb{R}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{M}^2 = {\rm diag}(0, \Delta m^2_{21}, \Delta m^2_{31}, \ldots)$, $\Delta m^2_{ij} \equiv m_i^2 - m_j^2$, $m_i$ and $m_j$ are the masses of the neutrino mass eigenstates $\nu_i$ and $\nu_j$, and $\mathbb{R}$ is the mixing matrix that rotates between the flavor and mass bases.  The matrix is parametrized by the rotation angles and CP-violation phases. At three neutrinos---the standard case---it corresponds to the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) mixing matrix, parametrized in terms of three mixing angles, $\theta_{12}$, $\theta_{23}$, and $\theta_{13}$, and one phase, $\delta_{\rm CP}$. 

In matter, neutrinos experience coherent forward scattering on matter via charged-current (CC) interactions. As a result, the Hamiltonian acquires the Mikheev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein (MSW) term\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1977ue, Mikheev:1986gs}
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h_matter}
 \mathbb{H}(E, l)
 =
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm vac}(E)
 +
 V_{\rm CC}(l)\, \mathbb{P} \;,
 \quad
 V_{\rm CC} = \sqrt{2}\, G_F\, n_e(l) \;,
\end{equation}
where $V_{\rm CC}$ is the matter potential, $n_e$ is the electron number density, $G_F$ is the Fermi constant, and $\mathbb{P}$, the projector, is a fixed matrix. The projector is where the flavor count enters. 

At three flavors, $\mathbb{P} = {\rm diag}(1,0,0)$: the neutral-current (NC) potential is common to the three active flavors and cancels from the probabilities. With a sterile state, it no longer cancels, since a sterile neutrino is a gauge singlet and feels neither potential. In this case, $\mathbb{P} = {\rm diag}(1, 0, 0, r/2, \ldots)$ with $r = n_n/n_p$ the ratio of neutrons to protons. The $r/2$ factor is explained as follows. The NC potential of an active flavor is $V_{\rm NC} = -G_F n_n/\sqrt{2}$. Subtracting it from the whole diagonal is what removes it from the active flavors; what it leaves on a sterile state is $-V_{\rm NC}$. With $n_p = n_e$ by charge neutrality, $-V_{\rm NC} = G_F n_n/\sqrt{2} = (n_n/n_p)\,G_F n_e/\sqrt{2} = (r/2)\,V_{\rm CC}$. So the sterile entry is not an interaction of the sterile state but the residue of the subtraction, and it is $1/2$ for isoscalar matter (\ie, matter with $n_n = n_p$). \magnus\ builds $\mathbb{P}$ in {\tt matter.matter\_potential\_projector}, for every flavor count. (\magnus\ is not limited to isoscalar matter, though; the user can feed their own Hamiltonian for non-isoscalar matter to the solver.)

For antineutrinos, the mixing matrix is conjugated \emph{and} the matter potential changes sign. The second operation is applied once, inside the potential constructor, and doing only one of the two returns a plausible, but wrong answer\ \cite{MagnusDocs}. To prevent this from happening, shipped functions include a \texttt{nubar} flag that, when activated, performs both operations.

\section{The method}
\label{sec:method}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{History and context}

The Magnus expansion is due to Magnus\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz} and has a large literature in numerical analysis, where it is one of the standard geometric integrators---methods that preserve a structure of the problem exactly rather than to within the truncation error\ \cite{Iserles:2000acta, Hairer:2006gni}. Reference~\cite{Blanes:2008xlr} reviews it, and \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit} derives the Gauss-Legendre \emph{collocation} integrators that \magnus\ uses by default---collocation meaning that the Hamiltonian is sampled at a few chosen points inside each step, here Gauss-Legendre nodes, and the scheme is built so that those samples deliver the full order. 

The expansion has been used for neutrino oscillations before. It was brought to two-flavor evolution in matter by D'Olivo and Oteo\ \cite{DOlivo:1990xs}, developed for realistic profiles by Ioannisian and Smirnov\ \cite{Ioannisian:2008ve}, and turned into a practical integrator for the three-flavor problem by Casas \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Casas:2016asi}, who reported gains of up to two orders of magnitude in speed over general-purpose differential-equation solvers. 

\textbf{What has not been available is a general-purpose, tested, documented implementation, which \magnus\ is.} It takes an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at an arbitrary number of flavors, chooses its own discretization from a requested tolerance, and places its slab edges on the discontinuities of a layered profile. It also says which of its six internal engines answered a call---the independent methods that can serve a request, listed in Section \ref{sec:engines}---and reports, rather than assuming, when the evidence for its own convergence is weak, naming how far from converged it stopped.  \magnus\ implement the Magnus expansion carried to order 10 (the maximum by default, extendable), the quadratures, the slab chain and its adaptive refinement, an exactly unitary matrix exponential, the composition with adiabatic transport for extreme accumulated phase, the closed-form decohered limit, and the engineering that makes a whole scan over neutrino energy or arrival direction one batched call rather than thousands of separate ones.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Where the method applies and where it does not}

The method, and its implementation in \magnus, asks for one thing only: \textbf{that the Hamiltonian be Hermitian}. That is what makes the evolution unitary and the system closed, so that the total number of neutrinos, summed across all flavors, is constant. Section \ref{sec:limitations} says what cases this requirement excludes; they include different forms of open systems, like invisible neutrino decay and decoherence. In those cases, other codes should be used, like \texttt{nuSQuIDS}~\cite{Arguelles:2021twb, NuSQuIDS}.

Convergence of the Magnus expansion is not a second requirement but a constraint on the slab width. It is the code that internally meets the requirement, not the user. The series, which we introduce formally in Sec.~\ref{sec:expansion}, converges absolutely provided $\int \lVert \mathbb{H} \rVert_2\, dl < \pi$ over the interval the expansion is applied to\ \cite{Moan:2008conv}, where $\lVert \cdot \rVert_2$ is the spectral norm; for a Hermitian matrix, it os the largest eigenvalue in modulus. The condition is sufficient rather than necessary, so a particular $\mathbb{H}$ may converge well beyond it. But $\pi$ cannot be replaced by anything larger: there is a Hamiltonian whose series diverges as soon as the integral reaches it. Therefore, it is the bound to choose slabs against. For a given tolerance, \magnus\ chooses the position and width of slabs: a long trajectory is cut into as many as the criterion needs, without user intervention. (The caller may also call \magnus\ with a specific number of slabs rather a requested tolerance.) Whenever the code fails to reach a requested tolerance---due to, \eg, having set up too low a number of maximum slabs that can be used---the code reports so.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{The Magnus expansion}
\label{sec:expansion}
Writing $\mathbb{A}(l) \equiv -i\,\mathbb{H}(l)$, \equ{schrodinger} reads $d\mathbb{U}/dl = \mathbb{A}(l)\,\mathbb{U}$. Where $\mathbb{A}$ commutes with itself at different positions (\ie, for neutrinos, in vacuum, uniform density), the solution is $\exp \left( \int \mathbb{A}\,dl \right)$; where it does not, that expression is wrong. The time-ordered exponential that replaces it is the Magnus expansion\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz}:
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:magnus}
 \mathbb{U}(l, l_0) = \exp\left[\Omega(l, l_0)\right] \;,
 \qquad
 \Omega = \sum_{k \geq 1} \Omega_k \;,
\end{equation}
\emph{exactly}, with the terms generated by a recursion in the Bernoulli numbers $B_j$ (in the $B_1 = -1/2$ convention)\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr},
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:recursion}
 \Omega_1 = \int_{l_0}^{l} \mathbb{A}\,ds \;,
 \quad
 \Omega_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{B_j}{j!} \int_{l_0}^{l} S_k^{(j)}\, ds \;,
\end{equation}
where $S_k^{(1)} = [\Omega_{k-1}, \mathbb{A}]$ and $S_k^{(j)} = \sum_{m=1}^{k-j} [\Omega_m, S_{k-m}^{(j-1)}]$. The time-ordering means that the earlier-time operators are applied first, \ie, are positioned to the right of later-time operators.

The first three terms read
\begin{align}
 \label{equ:omega123}
 \Omega_1 &= \int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds\, \mathbb{A}(s) \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega_2 &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds_1 \int_{l_0}^{s_1}\! ds_2\,
 \left[\mathbb{A}(s_1), \mathbb{A}(s_2)\right] \;, \\
 \Omega_3 &= \frac{1}{6}\int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds_1 \int_{l_0}^{s_1}\! ds_2
 \int_{l_0}^{s_2}\! ds_3 \Big(
 \left[\mathbb{A}_1, [\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_3]\right] \nonumber \\
 &\hspace{3.1cm} + \left[\mathbb{A}_3, [\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_1]\right]
 \Big) \;, \nonumber
\end{align}
with $\mathbb{A}_i \equiv \mathbb{A}(s_i)$. \ref{app:terms} gives the structure at any order. The odd Bernoulli numbers vanish for $j \geq 3$, so whole commutator groups drop out and only $j = 1, 2, 4, 6, \ldots$ contribute. Every term unrolls to a right-nested chain $[\Omega_{m_1}, [\Omega_{m_2}, \ldots [\Omega_{m_j}, \mathbb{A}]\ldots]]$ with $\sum_i m_i = k-1$, so the terms of the $j$-th group are indexed by the compositions of $k-1$ into $j$ positive parts and there are $\binom{k-2}{j-1}$ of them. Their number roughly doubles per order: $\Omega_7$ has $17$ terms and $\Omega_{10}$ has $129$.

In neutrino oscillations, the first term of the Magnus expansion is the one driving the standard slab approximation. Where $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on position, $\Omega_1 = -i\mathbb{H}\Delta l$ is the whole of the expansion, since every higher term is a nested commutator of $\mathbb{H}$ with itself and vanishes identically. Its exponential is then the exact evolution operator, not a truncation of one. Evaluating it is exactly the problem {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} solves in closed form: expanding $\mathbb{H}$ and $e^{-i\mathbb{H}L}$ in the generators of SU($n$) returns the probability from traces alone, without ever constructing an eigenvector, and exactly to round-off at every flavor count for which the eigenvalues can be written down. Composing one such operator per slab, in the order the neutrino meets them, is the standard slab treatment of a piecewise-constant profile. We take that correspondence further in Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}.
% , where the correspondence is an identity rather than an analogy: reading the density at each slab's midpoint makes the slab composition the order-2 Magnus method, term for term.

\magnus\ implements \equ{recursion} through order 10. The first six order are hard-coded inline in the simplified forms from \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr}. Beyond that ($k \geq 7$), terms are generated from the recursion.  A separate module, {\tt expansionterms}, derives the same terms symbolically in exact rational arithmetic at \emph{any} order and shares no code with the numerical core. The two implementations of the Magnus expansion agree, evidencing correctness. (Users interested in applying the Magnus expansion to other problems---in magnetic resonance, quantum optics, lattice gauge theory, or condensed matter---may repurpose this module.) \ref{app:tests} states that the test suite regenerates the hard-coded recursion coefficients and compares the two.

\subsection{Truncation is exactly unitary}
\label{sec:unitarity}

Because $\mathbb{H}$ is  Hermitian, $\mathbb{A} = -i\mathbb{H}$ is anti-Hermitian. The commutator of two anti-Hermitian matrices is again anti-Hermitian. Every $\Omega_k$ in \equ{recursion} is a real-coefficient combination of nested commutators of $\mathbb{A}$ with itself, so every $\Omega_k$ is anti-Hermitian, as is any partial sum. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:unitary}
 \Omega^\dagger = -\Omega
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \left[\exp(\Omega)\right]^\dagger \exp(\Omega) = \mathbb{1}
\end{equation}
for \emph{every} truncation order and every quadrature accuracy. Truncating early costs accuracy; it never costs unitarity. This is the sense in which the method is a geometric integrator: the exact solution lies in the unitary group. Truncating the series and evaluating its integrals numerically are both approximations, and neither leaves the group: $\exp(\Omega)$ is in it at any setting, rather than merely closing on it as the discretization is refined.

In practice, the guarantee of unitarity is only as good as the exponential that realizes it: a computed operator inherits it only if the routine that exponentiates $\Omega$ preserves it. A general-purpose Pad\'e routine does not: it knows nothing of the structure of its argument and returns a matrix that is unitary only to its own error tolerance, so the exactness is lost at the last step. \magnus\ takes a route that cannot lose unitarity. Because $\Omega$ is anti-Hermitian, $\mathbb{K} \equiv i\Omega$ is Hermitian, so it diagonalizes with a unitary $\mathbb{V}$, and the exponential follows, \ie,
\begin{align}
 \mathbb{K} &= \mathbb{V}\, {\rm diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\, \mathbb{V}^\dagger \;, \nonumber \\
 \label{equ:expm}
 \exp(\Omega) &= \mathbb{V}\, {\rm diag}\!\left(e^{-i\lambda_1}, \ldots, e^{-i\lambda_n}\right) \mathbb{V}^\dagger \;,
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbb{K}$, all real-valued. Real eigenvalues make the middle factor a diagonal of pure phases, $\lvert e^{-i\lambda} \rvert = 1$, hence unitary. Conjugating it by the unitary $\mathbb{V}$ preserves that, so $\exp(\Omega)$ is unitary by its form rather than to the accuracy of an approximation. Section \ref{sec:exponential} describes how the \magnus\ kernel evaluates \equ{expm} without an eigensolver at two and three flavors, where the eigenvalues have a usable closed form; four and five take the same equation through a general Hermitian eigendecomposition.

Neither route is unitary to the last bit, because $\mathbb{V}$ itself is computed in floating-point arithmetic. Measured in \magnus\ output, the deviation from unitarity, $\lVert \mathbb{U}^\dagger \mathbb{U} - \mathbb{1}\rVert$, is $4 \cdot 10^{-16}$ per evaluation and does not grow with the workload: over a stack of $4096$ the worst case reaches $4 \cdot 10^{-15}$ while the median stays where it was. The worst case moves from $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ to $1.6 \cdot 10^{-11}$ across four decades in the number of points, at two through five flavors. Thus, for all practical purposes, \magnus\ preserves unitarity.

\subsection{Slab width follows the profile, not the phase}
\label{sec:phase_vs_profile}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure*}[t!]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{phase_vs_profile.pdf}
 \caption{\label{fig:phase_vs_profile}\textbf{Cost and accuracy of a Magnus slab, at three flavors.} \emph{Top left}: the two matter-density profiles the other three panels use---the constant $3$~g~cm$^{-3}$ beside it, the exponential of central density $100$~g~cm$^{-3}$ and scale height $300$~km below---each at an electron fraction of $0.5$. \emph{Top right}: the deviation of one slab from an exact exponentiation of the same constant $\mathbb{H}$, against energy, with the accumulated phase $\Phi$ of \equ{phase} above. The series terminates at $\Omega_1$, so only arithmetic remains: it bottoms out near the unit round-off $\varepsilon$ (machine epsilon, $2.2 \cdot 10^{-16}$ in double precision) and rises along $\Phi\varepsilon$. \emph{Bottom left}: time per probability for the six Magnus configurations and for {\tt DOP853}, which is timed alongside them and is not the reference, everything run at one tolerance of $10^{-8}$ fixed before the sweep, with the slab count and the samples per slab left for the package to choose from it. Cost climbs in steps because the code refines in discrete stages, and what comes back against the reference spans $2.4 \cdot 10^{-7}$ to $1.5 \cdot 10^{-11}$. \emph{Bottom right}: the deviation from the reference against the number of slabs, at the fixed energy given in the panel. Every reference is Richardson-extrapolated three times in extended precision: fifty decimal digits for the right-hand panels, converged to $3 \cdot 10^{-20}$, and one solution per energy at thirty digits, converged past $10^{-11}$, for the bottom left panel. See Section~\ref{sec:phase_vs_profile} for details.}
\end{figure*}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure~\ref{fig:phase_vs_profile} shows the property that decides which problems suit the method: a slab must be narrow enough to follow the variation of the profile across it, while the phase it accumulates inside may be arbitrarily large. Two panels in \figu{phase_vs_profile} measure it, the accuracy of a single slab against the phase it spans and the cost of a probability against the same. Regarding what changing the truncation order buys at a fixed phase (or, equivalently, fixed energy), shown in the final panel, we defer to Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}.

In \figu{phase_vs_profile}, we use \magnus\ to compute standard, three-flavor probabilities of neutrinos propagating in constant-density matter and in an exponentially falling matter profile (similar to the matter profile in the Sun). We score every probability in the figure against an answer built to be far more accurate than either code. The path is cut into equal slabs, the density is read at the midpoint of each, and the exponentials are multiplied together---all of it in extended precision, so that rounding contributes nothing. The answer depends on how many slabs we use. We adopt Richardson extrapolation: doubling the slab count shrinks the error by a known factor, so two runs can be combined to cancel it, and three such combinations in succession remove the three largest error terms. We keep doubling the slab count until the last two levels of the extrapolation agree to better than a set amount, and we take that amount as the reference's own error: $3 \cdot 10^{-20}$ at fifty digits for the right-column panels, and $10^{-11}$ at thirty digits for the lower-left panel. No curve of \magnus\ is scored against a reference whose own error we have not measured. We take our reference to be as good an approximation to \textit{exact} as numerical implementation allows.

\smallskip

\textit{Why the sweep is in energy.}---The accumulated phase of \equ{phase} is set by the eigenvalue gap of $\mathbb{H}$, \equ{phase}. In \figu{phase_vs_profile}, its vacuum part scales as $1/E$ and its matter part does not (see Sec.~\ref{sec:recap}), so lowering the energy raises the phase while leaving the density profile untouched. That is what makes an energy sweep a test of the two against each other: one quantity moves and the other does not. The correspondence is not a pure $1/E$: across the lower left panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile}, from $20$ to $0.02$~GeV, a factor of a thousand in energy, $\Phi$ runs from $6$ to $952$ radians, a factor of about $160$---because the energy-independent matter term dominates the gap at the high-energy end. Each panel therefore carries $\Phi$ on a second axis above the energy.

\smallskip

\textit{A constant Hamiltonian costs one slab.}---If $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on $l$, then every commutator in \equ{recursion} vanishes identically, $\Omega = -i\mathbb{H}\Delta l$ exactly, so the expansion terminates at its first term. The evolution operator over the whole interval is one exponential, whatever the accumulated phase. The upper-right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile} tests this over six decades of phase. What grows is $\Phi\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ the double-precision unit round-off, which reaches $8.5 \cdot 10^{-10}$ over the same span. That is the accuracy with which the phase is representable. It has nothing to do with the expansion: a closed-form probability evaluated in double precision at the same phase carries the same accuracy. At the top of that span a double-precision exponentiation is itself in error by $2 \cdot 10^{-10}$, the same size as the deviation being measured, so a double-precision reference could not tell the two apart. This is why we carry the reference at fifty decimal digits.

\smallskip

\textit{A varying Hamiltonian costs slabs according to how fast it varies.}---The corrections $\Omega_2, \Omega_3, \ldots$ are built from commutators of $\mathbb{A}$ at different positions, and those vanish to the extent that $\mathbb{A}$ is the same at both. Therefore, a slab should ideally be narrow enough that the \emph{Hamiltonian}---and not necessarily the neutrino state---is nearly constant across it. Two quantities vary inside that slab, at different rates: the state rotates through $\Phi$ radians, while the Hamiltonian driving it changes only as the matter density does, which can be appreciably slower. A stepping solver propagates the state, so its steps must be short against an oscillation period and its cost grows with the phase. \magnus\ propagates the operator: inside a slab it integrates $\mathbb{H}$ alone, which carries no oscillation, and the exponential then produces all $\Phi$ radians at once. The slab width is determined by how fast $\mathbb{H}$ varies, not by how many oscillations fall inside it.

\figu{phase_vs_profile}, lower-left panel, shows this difference. We compare \magnus\ against a stand-alone Runge-Kutta order-8 solver, the \texttt{DOP853} algorithm as implemented in \texttt{scipy.integrate}, and score both against a reference computed in extended precision with \texttt{mpmath}. For the exponentially falling density profile, we sweep the energy from $20$ to $0.02$~GeV, which carries the accumulated phase from $6$ to $952$ radians. Every configuration---the step solver included---is held at the same tolerance of $10^{-8}$. The slab count and the samples per slab are left to \magnus\ to refine from that tolerance, which is how the code is expected to be used, and why the curves step rather than glide: refinement proceeds in discrete stages until two successive stages agree, so every appreciable jump in cost is a stage boundary rather than an artifact of how finely we sampled the energy.

{\tt DOP853} grows steeply with the phase, as $\Phi^{0.77}$, from about $5$ to $270$~ms across the sweep. The Gauss--Legendre configurations are nearly indifferent to it: orders 4 and 6 grow as roughly $\Phi^{0.2}$, from under $3$ to about $12$~ms, so the gap widens from a factor of $2$ at the low-phase end to about $20$ at the high one. The cost for \magnus\ is set by the profile and for a step solver, by the phase; so the harder the problem becomes in phase, the further apart the two move.

The gap widens even though \magnus\ is also the more accurate answer. A tolerance is a request rather than a guarantee, and the configurations answer it differently: asked for $10^{-8}$, {\tt DOP853} returns $2.4 \cdot 10^{-7}$ at worst, while every Magnus configuration comes in under the request, order 10 by nearly three orders of magnitude. The panel therefore compares cost at a common \emph{request}, at which \magnus\ is both the cheaper answer and the better one. The reference resolves those deviations with room to spare: each configuration's worst is more than ten times the reference's own error. Only at the quiet end do the highest orders fall below that floor, and of those we can say only that they are at least as accurate as the reference is. Both codes are timed producing the same object, the full evolution matrix. \magnus\ has no choice---$\exp(\Omega)$ delivers every column at once---and we give {\tt DOP853} the matching mode, a single integration covering all columns rather than one per column, which is also its faster one by about a factor of three.

\magnus\ and {\tt DOP853} both flatten at the low-phase, high-energy end. The floors they settle on are where the claim is decided: a slab count answers to the profile, a step count to the phase. Up to about $40$ radians the order-6 slab count does not move: it holds near a hundred and fifty slabs, never coarser than a tenth of the profile's scale height, while the phase grows sevenfold underneath it. That floor belongs to the profile and not to the refinement. On a constant profile at the same energy the same call settles on two slabs, and flattening the exponential tenfold in scale height more than halves the count. {\tt DOP853}'s floor moves the other way: steepening the profile at fixed energy \emph{lowers} its step count, since what it must resolve is the phase, and a steeper profile leaves less matter along the path to accumulate any. The two discretizations answer to different things.

Order 2 is the most expensive of the three Gauss--Legendre Magnus configurations (the others being order 4 and order 6). This is not because the phase drives it harder: its slab count grows as $\Phi^{0.32}$, the slowest of the three. It is expensive because it converges as $N_{\rm slabs}^{-2}$, so the requested tolerance takes thousands of slabs at any phase. Its curve humps in the middle of the panel, where the MSW resonance for $\Delta m^2_{31}$ can be hit on the trajectory. There, the eigenvectors of $\mathbb{H}$ turn fastest, so neighboring points commute least, the slabs must narrow and their count must grow, leading to longer computation times. The resonant density rises as the energy falls, and below $0.27$~GeV it exceeds the profile's central $100$~g~cm$^{-3}$, so the path never reaches resonance and the hump ends. Orders 4 and 6 meet the same resonance more weakly, a higher order converting the same local difficulty into fewer extra slabs.

The cumulative-quadrature configurations of Section \ref{sec:quadrature}---which sample $\mathbb{A}$ on a uniform grid inside each slab using Simpson's rule for Magnus orders 6, 8, and 10---cost more still. But this is not because of their slab counts, which track the phase near $\Phi^{0.45}$ as the collocation configurations ones do. Instead, What grows is the samples per slab: Gauss--Legendre holds at two throughout, while Simpson settles at sixty-odd and climbs as $\Phi^{0.4}$ to a ceiling of $500$. Thus, at the far-phase end, order-10 Magnus spends some $4 \cdot 10^{5}$ evaluations of $\mathbb{H}$ against $3 \cdot 10^{3}$ on the default path (order 6, Gauss--Legendre), over a minute per probability against about $12$~ms.

\smallskip

\textit{A striking consequence.}---A 5-MeV solar neutrino accumulates $\Phi \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{4}$ radians, more than ten times the range swept in \figu{phase_vs_profile}. There, the gap only widens. More slabs are not the answer: Sec.~\ref{sec:hybrid} describes the strategy that regime needs, and Sec.~\ref{sec:solar} the observable that removes the need to propagate the phase at all and return the average probability instead.

\subsection{Two quadrature rules for the Magnus expansion}
\label{sec:quadrature}

Evaluating the Magnus recursion, \equ{recursion}, means sampling $\mathbb{A}$ inside each slab; \magnus\ offers two families of quadrature rule to approximate the integrals in the recursion.

\smallskip

\textit{Collocation integrators (default).---}We do not evaluate \equ{recursion} as it stands. Each $\Omega_k$ there is a $k$-fold integral over a simplex of $(k-1)$-fold nested commutators, so the integration and the commutator count both grow steeply with $k$; the sixth-order truncation alone would call for four such integrals. The collocation rules replace them. Each $\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(p)}$ is a quadrature approximation to a \emph{truncation} of the series in \equ{magnus}, assembled from a few samples of $\mathbb{A}$ at Gauss-Legendre nodes together with a fixed set of commutators among those samples. Its label is the order $p$ that it attains, not the number of terms that it keeps,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:gl_definition}
 \Omega_{\rm GL}^{(p)}
 =
 \sum_{k=1}^{m_p} \Omega_k + \mathcal{O}\!\left(h^{p+1}\right) \;.
\end{equation}
Here $m_p = 1,\, 2,\, 4,\, 6$ for $p = 2,\, 4,\, 6,\, 8$. The remainder is the next pair of terms of \equ{magnus}, which is what fixes the order at $p$.

About the slab midpoint each $\Omega_k$ carries only odd powers of $h$, since reversing the traversal inverts $\mathbb{U}$ and so changes the sign of $\Omega$ term by term. A one-step method with that symmetry converges at an even order\ \cite{Hairer:2006gni}, so no odd-order scheme of this family exists; the superscripts of \equ{gl} run 2, 4, 6, 8 with nothing available between them. What the collocation nodes buy is the economy: three samples of $\mathbb{A}$ reproduce a four-term truncation, four samples a six-term one. \ref{app:terms} derives both the retained sets and the evenness from the sizes of the $\Omega_k$.

We use $\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(p)}$ exactly as \equ{magnus} uses $\Omega$. On the $j$-th slab, spanning $l_{j-1}$ to $l_j$ with width $h = l_j - l_{j-1}$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:slab_exponential}
 \mathbb{U}_j = \exp\left[\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(p)}(l_j, l_{j-1})\right] \;,
\end{equation}
one matrix exponential per slab, with the $\mathbb{U}_j$ time-ordered. Truncating the series inside the exponential leaves $\mathbb{U}_j$ exactly unitary, for the reason of Sec.~\ref{sec:unitarity}.

Following \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit}, Magnus orders 2, 4, 6, and 8 are reached from only one, two, three, or four evaluations of $\mathbb{A}$ per slab, taken at the Gauss-Legendre nodes, with the quadrature order matched exactly to the truncation order. \magnus\ implements all four. (They are \textit{not} the commutator-free family of \Refs\ \cite{Blanes:2006cf, Alvermann:2011cf}, which replaces every commutator by a product of exponentials of linear combinations of the $\mathbb{A}_i$.) With $h$ the slab width and $\mathbb{A}_j \equiv -i \mathbb{H}_j$ the Hamiltonian at node $j$, 
\begin{align}
 \label{equ:gl}
 \Omega_{\rm GL}^{(2)} &= h\, \mathbb{A}_1 \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega_{\rm GL}^{(4)} &= \frac{h}{2}\left(\mathbb{A}_1 + \mathbb{A}_2\right)
 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{12}\, h^2 \left[\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_1\right] \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega_{\rm GL}^{(6)} &= \alpha_1 + \frac{\alpha_3}{12}
 + \frac{1}{240} \left[-20\,\alpha_1 - \alpha_3 + \mathbb{C}_1,\,
 \alpha_2 + \mathbb{C}_2\right] \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega_{\rm GL}^{(8)} &= \alpha_1 + \frac{\alpha_3}{12}
 - \frac{7}{120}\,\mathbb{C}_3 + \frac{1}{360}\,\mathbb{C}_6 \;.
\end{align}
Orders 2 and 4 follow \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit}; orders 6 and 8 are the commutator-optimized forms of \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2002opt}, the order-6 one also reproduced in the review of \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr}. Orders 4, 6, and 8 need 1, 3, and 6 commutators, the fewest possible at each order\ \cite{Blanes:2002opt}. \tabl{collocation} collects the $\alpha_i$ and the $\mathbb{C}_i$ that build the last two of \equ{gl}; the two columns are different objects on different nodes, and cannot be mixed.

We have found no collocation scheme of this form at order 10. Reference\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit} only tabulates the size of the problem there---80 determining equations after time-symmetry, against 27 at order 8; \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2002opt} stops at order 8, as does the review of \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr}. The commutator-free family does not reach higher either, \Refs\ \cite{Blanes:2006cf, Alvermann:2011cf} optimizing up to order 8 as well. Order 8 appears to be where the optimized literature ends. However, given the decreasing decrease in speed and accuracy when moving from order 6 to 8 (see \figu{smooth_reach}), reaching for order 10 might be overkill in most settings.

Because the quadrature is matched to the truncation, collocation integration needs far fewer Hamiltonian evaluations than a general rule for the same accuracy. It is simultaneously the fastest and the most accurate choice whenever $\mathbb{A}$ is smooth inside a slab. This is the case inside the Earth, where slab edges are aligned to a layered profile (Sec.~\ref{sec:refinement}), with the density smooth between layer boundaries; the slab edges can be put on the boundaries themselves (and \magnus\ does, automatically).

\smallskip

\textit{Cumulative quadrature.---}The alternative rule is cumulative quadrature: \magnus\ samples $\mathbb{A}$ on a uniform grid of $m$ points inside each slab ($m = 50$ by default) and integrates with the trapezoid or Simpson rule, selectable. The points are $s_i = l_{j-1} + i\,\Delta s$, spaced by $\Delta s = h/(m-1)$. The label of this family counts the terms kept outright, where that of \equ{gl_definition} counts the order attained,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:cum_definition}
 \Omega_{\rm cum}^{(k)}
 =
 \sum_{j=1}^{k} \widehat{\Omega}_j \;,
\end{equation}
with each $\widehat{\Omega}_j$ the corresponding term of \equ{recursion} carrying sums over that grid in place of its integrals,
\begin{align}
 \label{equ:cum_weights}
 \widehat{\Omega}_1 &= \Delta s \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} w_i\, \mathbb{A}(s_i) \;, \nonumber \\
 \widehat{\Omega}_k &= \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{B_j}{j!}\, \Delta s \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} w_i\, S_k^{(j)}(s_i) \;,
\end{align}
where the $B_j$ are the Bernoulli numbers of \equ{recursion}, in the same $B_1 = -1/2$ convention, with the $S_k^{(j)}$ the nested-commutator combinations defined immediately below it. The $w_i$ are the composite trapezoid or Simpson weights (for Simpson, an even $m$ carries a corrected final interval rather than the textbook one). Every term up to the $k$-th is kept. The slab operator then follows as in \equ{slab_exponential},
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:slab_exponential_cum}
 \mathbb{U}_j = \exp\left[\Omega_{\rm cum}^{(k)}(l_j, l_{j-1})\right] \;,
\end{equation}
so the two families differ only in how that exponent is built. It is slower than the collocation integrators for the same accuracy on a smooth profile, but fully general. Therefore, it is the safer choice if $\mathbb{A}$ has a kink or a jump \emph{inside} a slab, where Gauss-Legendre loses its order advantage. It is also the only route above order 8, where the collocation path stops. 
% Requesting more from that path raises an error rather than quietly returning an order-8 result.

\smallskip

The two dials that select which quadrature rule to use are {\tt magnus\_exp\_order} and {\tt integration\_method}, the latter taking {\tt 'gl'} for the collocation rules above and {\tt 'simpson'} or {\tt 'trapezoid'} for the cumulative ones; \tabl{orders} lists what each delivers. \magnus\ defaults to {\tt 'gl'} and {\tt magnus\_exp\_order} = 4. The adaptive refinement of \magnus\ converts a higher order into fewer slabs on its own, so order and tolerance interact: raising the order pays only once the tolerance is tight enough to make the extra work per slab worthwhile. Measured across Earth, solar, and exponential-profile cases, order 6 runs $0.89$ to $1.04$ times the wall time of order 4 at a requested tolerance of $10^{-4}$, and $1.08$ to $1.93$ times \emph{faster} at $10^{-8}$. Dropping to order 2 is almost never worthwhile: at a tolerance of $10^{-8}$ on an Earth chord, it needs thousands of slabs, whereas order 6 needs about a hundred.

\subsection{Composition, and what a closed-form slab code is doing}
\label{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}

We cut a trajectory into $N$ slabs, evaluate the expansion independently in each, and compose the operators in position order,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:composition}
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm tot}
 =
 \mathbb{U}_N \cdots \mathbb{U}_2\, \mathbb{U}_1 \;,
\end{equation}
with the slab crossed \emph{first} standing \emph{rightmost}, since the operators act on the state to their right. Because each factor is unitary by construction, so is the product, however many factors there are and whatever the accuracy of each. The ordering matters physically whenever the slab Hamiltonians do not commute---an asymmetric profile together with a non-zero CP phase---and we check it in the test suite against a two-slab case done in matrix arithmetic alone, with no quadrature involved.

Equation (\ref{equ:composition}) is also where this method and a closed-form slab code meet. They meet more closely than either literature suggests. Take \equ{gl} at order 2: $\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(2)} = h\,\mathbb{A}_1$, and the single Gauss--Legendre node of a one-point rule is the slab \emph{midpoint}, so $\mathbb{U}_j = \exp[-i\,\mathbb{H}(l_j^{\rm mid})\,h]$. That is precisely a piecewise-constant composition with the density read at each slab's midpoint---the algorithm a closed-form slab code runs. The two are the same expression, as the lower right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile} confirms numerically. Over twenty-seven slab counts spanning four decades, the order-2 Magnus method and an independently written midpoint slab product return probability matrices agreeing to between $10^{-15}$ and $10^{-13}$. That is round-off, as the agreement of two evaluations of one formula should be, rather than the agreement of two methods.

A closed-form slab code applied to a continuously varying profile is already doing Magnus at order 2 without calling it that: the unfamiliar object reduces, at its lowest order, to the staircase of constant-density slabs that every closed-form code already runs, and what \magnus\ adds on that same staircase is orders 4, 6, and 8. Its second-order convergence in the slab width (halving the width buys a factor of four) is the order-2 truncation error, not an artifact of freezing the density. What \magnus\ contributes on the identical slab chain is the higher orders, whose errors fall by sixteen, sixty-four, and more per halving. At $1\,024$ slabs of the lower right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile}, order 2 stands at $4 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and order 4 at $10^{-10}$, while order 6 has already reached $4 \cdot 10^{-15}$. Order 2 does not reach $10^{-8}$ anywhere on the panel; order 6 is below it by $128$ slabs. It contributes, separately, the flavor counts and Hamiltonians no closed form can freeze, which is most of Section \ref{sec:examples}.

The two paths do not mean the same thing by {\tt magnus\_exp\_order}. Since it is the argument the caller sets, the mapping is worth stating outright. On the Gauss--Legendre path it is the classical order of the method, reached by the smallest collocation scheme that attains it. On the quadrature path it is instead the index of the last term $\Omega_k$ retained; the delivered order is $2\lfloor \mathrm{order}/2 \rfloor + 2$, which runs ahead of that index because the first omitted term is smaller than its position alone would suggest (\ref{app:terms}). Above order 8 the collocation schemes run out, so we evaluate order 10 on Simpson quadrature instead; the dash is the request the Gauss--Legendre path declines rather than silently answering at order 8. A request for order 6 therefore returns a sixth-order method on the default path and an eighth-order one on Simpson. Measured against the default path at that same label, the two extra orders are not a property of Simpson's rule: they come from $\Omega_5$ and $\Omega_6$, which the quadrature path retains and the collocation path does not. Order 3 on Simpson retains $\Omega_1 + \Omega_2 + \Omega_3$ and converges as $N^{-4}$. Order 6 on Gauss--Legendre retains $\Omega_4$ as well, which is what carries it to $N^{-6}$; it reaches that from three samples of $\mathbb{A}$ rather than from a grid.

Two errors compete along each curve in that panel. The truncation error falls as $N^{-p}$ at order $p$, while the round-off of the composition grows with $N$, so their sum passes through a minimum and then flattens onto a floor the panel shows as a plateau. Order 2 is the exception: its truncation error is still the larger of the two at $16\,384$ slabs, so it falls as $N^{-2}$ across the whole panel and reaches $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$ without ever turning. The others meet their minimum sooner the higher the order (at $8\,192$ slabs for order 4, $1\,448$ for order 6 on Gauss--Legendre, $724$ for order 6 on Simpson, and $362$ for orders 8 and 10) and settle onto a common floor near $7 \cdot 10^{-14}$.

The floor belongs to no order. Composing $N$ slabs multiplies $N$ double-precision unitary factors, each carrying a relative error of order $\varepsilon$; those accumulate incoherently. We isolate the multiplication alone by composing the \emph{same} slab operators once in double precision and once at fifty digits, so that the truncation cancels exactly: the difference is $1.5 \cdot 10^{-16}$ at two slabs and $1.6 \cdot 10^{-14}$ at $16\,384$, a growth close to $\sqrt{N}$. The floor the curves reach sits a few times above that, because each factor is itself formed in double precision before it is multiplied in. The panel shows the same thing directly: beyond about a thousand slabs, orders 6, 8 and 10 on Simpson return \emph{identical} numbers at every plotted point. Refining past the minimum costs accuracy rather than buying it, so the useful range of $N$ ends where the plateau begins, which is where the refinement ladder stops.

\subsection{Adaptive refinement, and what a tolerance means}
\label{sec:refinement}

By default we do not leave the slab count to the caller. We compute the probability matrix, grow the number of slabs, recompute, and stop when two successive levels agree within ${\tt atol} + {\tt rtol}\lvert P \rvert$. We call that sequence of increasingly fine grids the \emph{refinement ladder}. The call is charged for the whole of it, not only for the count it settles on: measured on an Earth chord at three flavors, reaching a given accuracy through the ladder costs about four times what the same accuracy costs at a slab count fixed in advance, and the factor holds from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-10}$. A caller who has calibrated that count once can set {\tt rtol} and {\tt atol} to {\tt None} and pay only for the answer, giving up the agreement between successive levels that the ladder otherwise supplies. Four further devices make it efficient.

\smallskip

\textit{A physics-informed starting count.}---Rather than start from one slab, we seed the ladder from an estimate of the accumulated traceless phase $\lVert \Omega_1 \rVert_2$ over the whole trajectory, aiming for roughly $2\pi$ radians per slab, which puts the Gauss--Legendre method close to converged on its first attempt.

\smallskip

\textit{Warm starts across a scan.}---In an energy scan or an oscillogram, each point's refinement is seeded from the previous point's converged counts rather than reclimbing the ladder.

\smallskip

\textit{Slab edges on the discontinuities.}---The PREM profile\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy} is piecewise smooth with density jumps at nine shell boundaries. A slab straddling one of them degrades locally to low order no matter how high the truncation order is set. The effect is severe enough to invert the usual reasoning about accuracy: placing the edges correctly matters more than the order of the integrator.  On a core-crossing chord at $\cos\theta_z = -0.85$ and $5$~GeV, with the edges spaced uniformly, orders 2, 4 and 6 all stall together near $1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}$ and stop improving---at $800$ slabs they lie within $15\%$ of each other, with order 2 the most accurate of the three at $200$ slabs. 

Declaring the sixteen points at which that chord crosses a shell boundary---it enters and leaves most of them---separates the orders cleanly: at $800$ slabs the three orders reach $2.8 \cdot 10^{-8}$, $2.1 \cdot 10^{-13}$ and $7.1 \cdot 10^{-14}$, the last being the floor of the reference rather than of the method. Raising the order buys nothing at all until the discontinuities are respected. The Earth entry points solve a quadratic in the arrival direction for the exact chord positions of every boundary crossing and insert them as mandatory slab edges at every refinement level. A caller with a jump of their own (a shock front, say, or a tabulated profile with a kink) passes its position through {\tt t\_breakpoints} and gets the same treatment. Measured over a population of piecewise-constant profiles, declaring the edges moves the median error from $7.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$ to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-12}$.

\smallskip

\textit{A caller-supplied floor.}---Passing a slab count together with a tolerance sets a lower bound on the ladder rather than replacing it, so knowledge about the profile can be added without giving up the refinement, which continues upward from the floor the caller set rather than starting again from a single slab.

\smallskip

\textbf{The tolerances are a stopping criterion, not an error bound.} Nothing in the loop estimates the error of the answer it returns; convergence is \emph{inferred} from agreement between successive levels. Usually that is conservative---for a sequence converging as $C N^{-p}$ the level-to-level gap overstates the error of the finer level---but agreement is evidence, not proof. On a sawtooth density, two successive levels agreed with each other and the returned answer was wrong by $0.855$ in probability. A {\tt strict\_convergence} option requires two \emph{consecutive} agreements for that reason.

Richardson extrapolation---combining two answers at different step sizes so that the leading error term cancels, leaving both a better answer and an estimate of what was canceled---would convert that gap into an error estimate, as {\tt NuOscProbExact} does with its slab count. The observed convergence order is not stable enough to divide by. Fitting it on an Earth chord against a $4096$-slab reference gives $p = 3.84$, $5.62$ and $4.06$ at $1$, $2$ and $10$~GeV for a nominal order of four, and $1.15$, $2.66$ and $1.59$ for a nominal order of two---scattered by more than a factor of two, one sequence not even monotone. Assuming $p$ equals the requested order would divide the gap by too large a denominator wherever the true order is lower, reporting an error \emph{smaller} than the truth. Reporting too small an error is the dangerous direction to be wrong in, so we decline to report one at all.

On a medium with structure below the grid---a turbulent or noisy profile---do not expect the ladder to discover the scale. Set {\tt n\_slabs} from the shortest wavelength carrying power near an eigenvalue splitting, and it is honored as a \emph{floor} at every refinement level, leaving the ladder free to add slabs above it. Where a refinement cap is reached before the tolerance is met, \magnus\ returns its best estimate and raises a warning unconditionally, naming how far from converged it stopped as a multiple of the tolerance. The returned probabilities remain unitary to round-off, so they can look entirely plausible while being inaccurate---which is the reason the warning is unconditional. Section \ref{sec:diagnostics} reports how often those warnings fire, and how often an answer comes back wrong without one.

\subsection{The matrix exponential}
\label{sec:exponential}

Every slab ends in an exponential, whose cost at these sizes is not incidental. {\tt numpy.linalg.eigh} costs about $1.27~\mu$s per $3\times3$ whatever the stack size---measured $1.268~\mu$s at $N = 108$ and $1.279~\mu$s at $N = 4096$, flat, because it loops over LAPACK internally rather than vectorizing over the stack. We therefore carry a compiled Cayley--Hamilton kernel for $2\times2$ and $3\times3$, which applies to $\mathbb{K}$ the polynomial interpolating $e^{-i\lambda}$ on its spectrum: no eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues in closed form. Interleaved against {\tt eigh} with a control the change cannot touch, the kernel is $6.8$ times faster on the exponential alone at $N = 108$ and $6.9$ at $N = 4096$ ($13.2$ at two flavors), and $2.11$ times faster end to end on a $60$-energy PREM scan.

Accuracy, not only speed, follows from taking that route\ \cite{Zaglauer:1988gz}. Closed-form evaluations of the evolution operator reconstruct it from the eigenvalues through \emph{divided differences}, the finite-difference ratios $[f(\lambda_j) - f(\lambda_k)]/(\lambda_j - \lambda_k)$ that interpolate a function on a set of nodes, every such construction dividing at some point by a quantity that vanishes as two eigenvalues meet, so the digits they return degrade as a spectrum approaches degeneracy. The Cayley--Hamilton kernel does divide by eigenvalue gaps---the divided differences it interpolates with are built from them---but it divides in a form that stays bounded: each appears as a sinc, whose \emph{error} vanishes with the gap rather than growing, and the eigenvalues are sorted so that the cancellation happens between neighbors. That is why it needs no tolerance, no crossover and no near-degenerate branch, and why the measured error is flat where a naive eigenvector construction would break. The consequence is measurable. On random Hermitian Hamiltonians whose two closest eigenvalues are driven together by hand, checked against a $60$-digit reference, the error is flat across ten decades of relative gap---a median of $4 \cdot 10^{-14}$ at an accumulated phase of $10^3$ and $2 \cdot 10^{-12}$ at $10^5$, with a relative gap from $10^{-1}$ down to $10^{-11}$ making no difference to either. A near-degenerate spectrum is a hard case for a closed form; here it is an unremarkable one, reached with no parameter tuned to make it so.

The gap between $6.8$ and $2.11$ is Amdahl's law---a speedup applied to one part of a calculation is diluted by the part it does not touch---rather than a disappointment: the exponential is roughly a third of a slab pass, so removing six sevenths of a third is about what the end-to-end number shows. Quoting the $6.8$ as a package speed-up would be quoting the wrong number. Dimensions four and five keep {\tt eigh}. The $5\times5$ characteristic polynomial is a quintic, with no solution in radicals at all; the quartic has one, but not a form worth evaluating at these sizes (however, \texttt{NuOscProbExact} does evaluate it).

One correction we make in public because it was missed once. The closed form was first verified against random spectra at many norms, and separately against many eigenvalue separations at norm near one. Where those two conditions hold \emph{together} it reached $2.7 \cdot 10^{-7}$ against {\tt eigh}'s $3.0 \cdot 10^{-11}$ (a factor of $7440$) because $\arccos$ has infinite derivative at $u = \pm 1$. Neither single-axis sweep visits that corner. The kernel now hands such matrices to {\tt eigh}; the worst absolute error over the whole separation-by-scale grid is $8.7 \cdot 10^{-14}$; the fraction of matrices declined on real work---PREM chords, solar slab chains, constant density, non-standard interactions---measures $0.00\%$. Switching backend moves probabilities by at most $4.6 \cdot 10^{-15}$ across all of those.

\subsection{Adiabatic transport with exact Magnus patches}
\label{sec:hybrid}

Section \ref{sec:phase_vs_profile} showed the cost growing slowly with accumulated phase, not vanishing. For a few-MeV solar neutrino crossing most of the Sun, $\Phi$ reaches $10^{3}$--$10^{6}$ radians and the slab ladder can exhaust its cap before the tolerance is met. Physically, though, nothing there is happening quickly: away from a level crossing the flavor content follows the instantaneous eigenstates of $\mathbb{H}$ adiabatically, which is exactly what the Mikheyev--Smirnov--Wolfenstein description of solar neutrinos says\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1977ue, Mikheev:1986gs, Parke:1986jy, Bethe:1986ej, Kuo:1989qe}.

\magnus\ automates that. Diagonalizing $\mathbb{H}(l) = \mathbb{W}(l)\,{\rm diag}(\lambda_k)\,\mathbb{W}^\dagger(l)$ at each position, adiabatic transport is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:adiabatic}
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm ad}(l_1, l_0)
 =
 \mathbb{W}(l_1)\,{\rm diag}\!\left(e^{-i\Phi_k(l_1)}\right)\mathbb{W}^\dagger(l_0) \;,
\end{equation}
with $\Phi_k = \int \lambda_k\,ds$ the dynamical phase, integrated by Simpson's rule over a grid of diagonalized Hamiltonians. It never resolves the accumulated phase step by step, because $\Phi_k$ is a smooth integral however large it becomes. The geometric (Berry) phase needs no separate formula: at each grid step the columns of $\mathbb{W}$ are phase-fixed by maximizing the real, positive overlap with the previous step's, a discrete parallel transport that captures it implicitly, and correctly for genuinely complex Hamiltonians. The result is exactly unitary (a diagonal phase conjugated by unitary matrices) independently of how fine the grid is, so refining it buys accuracy without ever costing unitarity, a property no refinement level can trade away.

Where adiabaticity fails, we repair it rather than approximate it. Non-adiabatic windows are located with the Hellmann--Feynman identities\ \cite{Feynman:1939zza}
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:hf}
 \frac{d\lambda_k}{dl} = \langle v_k \lvert \tfrac{d\mathbb{H}}{dl} \rvert v_k \rangle ,
 \;\;
 \gamma_{jk} = \frac{\lvert\langle v_j \lvert \tfrac{d\mathbb{H}}{dl} \rvert v_k \rangle\rvert}
 {(\lambda_k - \lambda_j)^2} \;,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{jk}$ is the adiabaticity parameter of the level pair $(j,k)$: small where the eigenvalues are well separated compared with how fast the Hamiltonian is turning, and large where they approach. Both need only the derivative of the smooth, gauge-independent Hamiltonian and never that of an eigenvector (defined only up to a phase). Every pair $(j,k)$ is scanned, at any dimension, so no number of simultaneous resonances defeats the search. A window is grown outward from each candidate until $\gamma_{jk}$ falls back below threshold, which makes it a physical width rather than an artifact of the search grid. Overlapping windows are merged. Inside each we compute the evolution operator with the package's own Magnus kernel, refined until two levels agree.

We then compose the pieces by the exact law
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:hybrid_composition}
 \mathbb{U}
 =
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm ad}\,\mathbb{U}_{\rm patch}\, \cdots \,\mathbb{U}_{\rm ad} \;,
\end{equation}
which holds regardless of which method computed each factor, and is unitary because each factor is. The crossing is therefore treated exactly rather than by a Landau--Zener formula\ \cite{Zener:1932ws}. As a check, the computed hop probability reproduces the analytic Landau--Zener value to a few parts in a thousand for a linear crossing. Nothing in the implementation assumes that formula, so the agreement tests the crossing rather than restating an expression the code never evaluates.

None of the thresholds involved has a value that is safe for an arbitrary Hamiltonian, so the strategy never trusts a single evaluation: it repeats the whole computation with the threshold tightened and both grids doubled, and reports the result as \emph{certified} only once two successive levels agree. Failing that, it returns its best estimate---still unitary to round-off, since that property does not depend on the grid---and says it is not certified. Against a tight {\tt DOP853} ground truth across a validation grid spanning zero, one, two separated and two merged resonances, at three, four and five flavors, real and CP-violating, the strategy is $30$ to $4800$ times faster with a maximum absolute error of $2.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and unitarity between $10^{-16}$ and $10^{-12}$. That maximum is taken over every return on the grid, certified or not, and against a requested $10^{-4}$; \ref{sec:limitations} reports separately how often a certified answer is nonetheless outside tolerance, which is the number that bounds what the strategy may be trusted for. Both blind spots are properties of a fixed grid rather than of this implementation; Section \ref{sec:limitations} gives the measurement behind each.

\subsection{The phase-averaged limit, in closed form}
\label{sec:averaged}

A neutrino from an astrophysical source arrives with a phase nobody resolves. For a TeV neutrino from $100$~Mpc the accumulated phase is of order $10^{15}$ radians; no ingredient of that number (the distance, the size of the production region, the detector's energy resolution) is known to anything approaching the precision the phase would demand. Whatever the true phase, the measurement integrates over many complete cycles of it. Propagating such a probability is therefore both expensive and beside the point.

Writing the amplitude in the eigenbasis of $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{V}\,{\rm diag}(\lambda_i)\,\mathbb{V}^\dagger$ and averaging over the phase kills every interference term, leaving
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_i \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}\rvert^2\, \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\beta i}\rvert^2 \;.
\end{equation}
This is not an approximation to be refined: it is the exact $L/E \to \infty$ limit, costing one matrix product rather than an integration. Three consequences follow immediately. It is symmetric in $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$; it is identical for neutrinos and antineutrinos \emph{in vacuum}, since there $\lvert \mathbb{V}^\ast \rvert^2 = \lvert \mathbb{V} \rvert^2$; in matter $\bar{\mathbb{H}}$ is not the conjugate of $\mathbb{H}$ and the two differ, so CP violation does not survive the average even though $\delta_{\rm CP}$ still enters through the magnitudes; and in vacuum it depends on neither energy nor baseline, since scaling $\mathbb{H}$ by $1/E$ leaves its eigenvectors alone.

Coherence is a physical question, and we treat it as one. Equation (\ref{equ:averaged}) assumes every \emph{relative} phase has averaged away, which is a statement about pairs of eigenvalues rather than about the spectrum as a whole. Two eigenvalues close enough to keep their relative phase fixed stay coherent and their cross term survives, so the implementation groups the spectrum into blocks of mutually coherent eigenvalues and sums coherently inside each,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged_blocks}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_b \Big\lvert \sum_{i \in b} \mathbb{V}^\ast_{\alpha i}\mathbb{V}_{\beta i} \Big\rvert^2 \;,
\end{equation}
which reduces to \equ{averaged} when every block is a singleton. A sterile state with a small $\Delta m^2_{41}$, or an exactly degenerate spectrum, makes the naive sum quietly wrong---with all eigenvalues equal the correct answer is the identity, nothing having oscillated, while \equ{averaged} returns a spurious mixture. Where a pair sits between the two limits, neither expression describes the result. The library says so rather than returning a number the physics does not support.

Two things this limit is not. It is not the partial averaging that a finite resolution produces: a spread of baselines or energies narrow enough to suppress an interference term without removing it is treated by damping factors, which deform the coefficients of the probability while leaving its frequencies alone, and which handle finite coherence, wave-packet separation and detector resolution on one footing\ \cite{Kiers:1995zj, Blennow:2005yk, Ohlsson:2000mj}. That is an operation on the assembled probability rather than on the propagation, which \magnus\ does not perform. Nor is \equ{averaged_blocks} the same as smearing over reconstructed energy, even in the fully averaged limit, because $\mathbb{H}$ itself depends on the energy: averaging over a band moves between Hamiltonians rather than selecting among the frequencies of one. In vacuum the two coincide, the eigenvectors being energy-independent; in matter near a resonance they do not. A user who takes the output of {\tt average=True} for a detector-resolution average will be wrong by an amount that grows with the density the neutrino has crossed.

Where the Hamiltonian varies there is no single eigenbasis to decohere in. A neutrino produced at $l_0$ decoheres in the eigenbasis \emph{there}, is carried along the levels, and is detected in the eigenbasis at $l_1$:
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged_varying}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_{ij} \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}(l_0)\rvert^2\,
 P^{\rm cross}_{ij}\,
 \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\beta j}(l_1)\rvert^2 \;,
\end{equation}
the standard MSW-plus-decoherence result\ \cite{Kuo:1989qe, Dighe:1999bi}, generalized here to any number of levels and any number of crossings. $P^{\rm cross}$ is the identity wherever adiabaticity holds, and departs from it only across a non-adiabatic window, whose transfer matrix is computed with the convergence-checked Magnus patch of Section \ref{sec:hybrid}---exactly, rather than by an asymptotic formula.

We show what this is worth on the Sun in Section \ref{sec:solar}, and why estimating the same quantity by averaging a scan does not converge in Section \ref{sec:averaging_is_not_estimating}.

\subsection{Scope and interface}
\label{sec:scope_interface}

\magnus\ is written entirely in Python and requires version 3.10 or later. Its dependencies ({\tt NumPy}\ \cite{Harris:2020xlr}, {\tt SciPy}\ \cite{Virtanen:2019joe}, {\tt joblib}, {\tt Numba}\ \cite{Lam:2015numba} and {\tt Matplotlib}\ \cite{Hunter:2007ouj}) are installed with it. We import {\tt Matplotlib} lazily inside the plotting helpers, so {\tt import magnus} does not pay for it.

The primary entry point is {\tt osc\_prob}. Its argument is a Hamiltonian: any callable ${\tt H\_func}(l)$ returning a Hermitian matrix of any size, or a constant matrix. Everything else in the public interface builds one. Around it we provide sixty-six explicitly named wrappers, covering every combination of flavor count ($2$, $3$, $4$, $5$), environment (vacuum, constant density, exponential density, the Earth through PREM, the Sun) and scenario (standard, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation). They are named to a rule, {\tt osc\_prob\_$\langle n\rangle$nu\_$\langle$environment$\rangle$\_$\langle$scenario$\rangle$}, so that {\tt osc\_prob\_3nu\_sun} and {\tt osc\_prob\_4nu\_earth\_liv} can be guessed rather than looked up; the scenario is omitted for the standard case. A user working on a standard case then passes named parameters ({\tt s12}, {\tt eps\_em}, {\tt rho}) rather than assembling dictionaries. Where a call is malformed the library says why rather than raising a bare exception: omitting the baseline on an Earth chord returns ``since two locations on the surface of the Earth have not been given, the value of {\tt costhz} will be used to define the chord length, but the baseline, {\tt L}, cannot be {\tt None}'', and a misspelled keyword returns the nearest match together with the full list of keywords the wrappers forward. Section \ref{sec:architecture} describes the three layers this is organized into and the contract between them. It is also why the listings below pass oscillation parameters two ways: a named wrapper takes them as loose keywords ({\tt **osc}), while {\tt osc\_prob\_matter\_std\_potential} while the middle-layer entry points take the dictionary itself.

Every entry point takes arrays. An energy scan, a set of baselines, or an oscillogram in energy and arrival direction is one call, and three structures let a batch cost less than its parts: a separable Hamiltonian shares one sampling of the potential across every energy, a potential that does not vary collapses a whole scan into a single stacked exponential, and many baselines at one energy are prefixes of the longest. Section \ref{sec:engines} describes the engines that exploit them and the order they are tried in. Section \ref{sec:engines} lists the six engines and the order they are tried in. Any call can be asked which one answered it.

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows how the parts of \magnus\ stand to one another. The flow runs left to right, from a Hamiltonian to a probability, and the interesting structure is in the middle: a request arrives at {\tt oscprob} and leaves it by one of three routes, only one of which walks slabs at all. Which route it takes is chosen from the request rather than by the caller, and Section \ref{sec:engines} sets out the order in which the choice is made.

Neither of the two properties that follow is visible from outside the package. The first is that {\tt magnus} imports nothing from the rest of the package. It is numerical linear algebra on an arbitrary matrix function $\mathbb{A}(l)$, with no notion of a neutrino anywhere in it, so it can be tested against the Bernoulli recursion without any of the probability machinery being involved. The second is the converse: {\tt oscprob} is the only module that imports every other one. Wiring the physics, the environments and the numerical core together happens deliberately in one place rather than a little at a time across the modules it draws on.

\tabl{modules} says what each module of \magnus\ is for. Twelve of them sit under {\tt src/magnus/}, together with the {\tt hamiltonians} subpackage, each carrying a single responsibility that no other module shares. {\tt magnus.oscprob} is the largest of them, because it exposes an explicitly named function for every combination of flavor count, environment and scenario---about sixty of them. To keep that from becoming sixty copies of the same logic, which is exactly what once let several bugs hide, every one of those functions is a thin call into a much smaller set, in three layers. Layer one is {\tt osc\_prob}, the only function that calls the Magnus core, owning the refinement loop, validation and logging, and itself a first-class public entry point for an arbitrary Hamiltonian. Layer two is four scenario functions---vacuum, standard matter, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation---each generic in the flavor count. Layer three is the named wrappers, whose entire job is to repackage named parameters into the dictionaries layer two expects.

The contract between the layers is enforced rather than documented: a wrapper must \emph{not} redeclare any of the fifteen refinement and logging keyword arguments that layers one and two own; two permanent tests inspect every wrapper's signature and fail if it does. Before that contract existed, every wrapper declared its own copy of those parameters with its own defaults, and fixing a default meant remembering to fix it in sixty places. Inevitably some were missed: a wrapper with a silently different default tolerance than its siblings, a wrapper missing the antineutrino flag entirely, a wrapper whose validation bound disagreed with its siblings. One consequence is worth stating for a reader at the console: the refinement keywords---{\tt n\_slabs}, {\tt max\_n\_slabs}, {\tt t\_breakpoints}, {\tt magnus\_exp\_order}, {\tt integration\_method}, {\tt rtol}, {\tt atol}, {\tt strict\_convergence}, {\tt n\_jobs}---are declared by {\tt osc\_prob} and reach a wrapper through {\tt **kwargs}, so {\tt help()} on the wrapper does not list them and tab completion does not offer them. Each wrapper's docstring names them and points at {\tt osc\_prob}, which is where their meanings live.

Extending the package means writing a Hamiltonian, and usually nothing else. {\tt osc\_prob} accepts any callable returning a Hermitian matrix of any size and does not ask where it came from, so a new term is added to whatever an existing builder returns---an ordinary array---which is then passed on unchanged. Only genuinely new physics---a term that fits none of vacuum, matter, non-standard interactions or Lorentz violation---calls for a new layer-two function.

\subsection{The six engines}
\label{sec:engines}

A user asks for a probability, not for a method. Behind the interface of Section \ref{sec:scope_interface}, six engines can answer a request. Every entry point tries them in one fixed order, taking the first whose assumptions the request actually satisfies. That order runs from the most specialized engine to the most general. An engine that cannot honestly serve a request declines it; the request then falls through to the next one down, until it reaches the general Magnus ladder at the bottom of the list, which accepts whatever arrives and never declines.

Figure~\ref{fig:strategies} sets the six side by side in that order, so that what separates them---how much of the trajectory each one walks, and whether it walks any---can be seen at once rather than reconstructed from the list below.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \emph{Closed-form average.} Some experiments do not measure an oscillation. They measure what is left of one: a solar neutrino arrives after a baseline so long, and with an energy resolution so broad, that the oscillating terms in the probability average away. What survives is an incoherent sum over mass eigenstates. When a caller asks for that averaged quantity and $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on position, no propagation is needed at all. We diagonalize $\mathbb{H}$ once and form $\langle P_{\alpha\beta}\rangle = \sum_i |V_{\alpha i}|^2 |V_{\beta i}|^2$ from the eigenvectors alone (Section \ref{sec:averaged}), which is exact for the averaged observable. No baseline enters the calculation; nothing is integrated. We call this a closed-form average because the averaged probability follows algebraically once the eigenbasis is known. The eigenbasis itself is still obtained numerically, so this is not a closed-form expression in the mixing parameters of the kind available for two flavors in vacuum, where the whole probability collapses to a single sine squared in $L/E$ with a constant amplitude.

\item \emph{Adiabatic $+$ Magnus.} If the matter profile varies slowly enough, a neutrino that starts in one instantaneous eigenstate of $\mathbb{H}(l)$ stays in it. The whole evolution is then a dynamical phase---the integral of the eigenvalues along the trajectory---together with a geometric phase. Both can be computed on a coarse grid however large the accumulated phase becomes: a slab method pays for oscillation phase, whereas this one does not. The approximation fails only over the narrow window around an MSW resonance, where two eigenvalues approach each other and the eigenstates rotate quickly.

There the engine falls back on the package's own integrator. It solves that short window with an exact Magnus computation, then stitches the pieces together with the composition law $U(l_2, l_0) = U(l_2, l_1)\,U(l_1, l_0)$, so the result is exactly unitary whatever the accuracy of the approximation. Where a patch is needed is decided by an exact Hellmann--Feynman diagnostic on the eigenvalue gaps rather than by finite-differencing eigenvectors, which are gauge-ambiguous. The test therefore works at any flavor count, with any number of resonances along the trajectory (Section \ref{sec:hybrid}). The engine finally certifies itself, refining each of its internal tolerances and checking that the answer does not move. It declines when the caller supplies slab edges or breakpoints, when no tolerance was requested, when the profile fails its resolution test, and when it runs but cannot certify the answer it produced, in which case the request falls through untouched to the engine below it.

\item \emph{Interaction picture.} At low energy the vacuum oscillation phase is enormous. A slab method must resolve every cycle of it, even though that part of the problem already has an exact solution. This engine removes it from the integration. For a Hamiltonian of the separable form $\mathbb{H}(E, l) = \mathbb{H}_E(E) + V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}$, the first term does not depend on position, so we diagonalize it once. In its eigenbasis the free evolution $e^{-i \mathbb{H}_E s}$ is a known diagonal phase for any distance $s$, which we factor out analytically instead of resolving it with narrow slabs. What is left to integrate is only the matter envelope $V_{\rm CC}(l)$, which varies on the scale height of the profile rather than on that of the oscillation.

When that envelope is a genuine exponential, $V_{\rm CC}(l_0 + s) = V_{\rm CC}(l_0)\,e^{-s/l_{\rm scale}}$, the first-order Magnus term of the resulting generator has a closed form. Over a slab of length $h$ that begins at position $l_0$, that first-order term reads
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:ip_omega1}
 \big(\tilde{\Omega}_1\big)_{jk}(h)
 = -i\, \big(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\big)_{jk}\, V_{\rm CC}(l_0)\,
 \frac{e^{(i \Delta_{jk} - 1/l_{\rm scale})h} - 1}{i \Delta_{jk} - 1/l_{\rm scale}} ,
\end{equation}
where a tilde marks a matrix written in the eigenbasis of $\mathbb{H}_E$, the indices $j$ and $k$ run over its eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, the gaps are $\Delta_{jk} = \lambda_j - \lambda_k$, and $l_{\rm scale}$ is the scale height of the density profile. The expression holds for $j = k$ as well, since that denominator is $-1/l_{\rm scale}$, which never vanishes for any finite scale height, so one expression serves the diagonal and the off-diagonal entries alike.

The closed form is exact in the envelope: we make no piecewise-constant or piecewise-linear approximation of $V_{\rm CC}$. The only approximation left is truncating the interaction-picture series at first order, which is accurate wherever the matter term is a small perturbation on the vacuum splitting. That truncation \emph{improves} as the energy falls, because $\Delta_{jk}$ grows as $1/E$; the regime that is hardest for a slab method is therefore the easiest for this one. The engine serves a single point as readily as a scan over energies at a shared baseline. It declines for more than two flavors, for a profile not tagged as exponential, for supplied slab edges, and when its own iteration fails to converge---which is what happens at an MSW resonance, where the matter term becomes comparable to the vacuum splitting rather than small. The first-order truncation then loses its justification, which the engine detects and reports.

  An exponential profile is also the one case in which an independent analytic answer exists to check against. Eliminating one flavor amplitude from the two-flavor evolution equation leaves a second-order equation for the other. The substitution that removes its first-derivative term puts that equation into a standard form whose solvability depends only on the shape of $V_{\rm CC}(l)$\ \cite{Notzold:1987cq, Kim:1987ss}. An exponential profile carries it to Whittaker's equation, so the two-flavor probability closes in confluent hypergeometric functions\ \cite{Petcov:1987xd, Toshev:1987jw, Petcov:1987zj}; a linear profile gives parabolic cylinder functions instead, which is the setting of the original crossing calculation\ \cite{Zener:1932ws}. That the solvable profiles form so short a list is not accidental: Balantekin showed that they are the ones obeying a shape-invariance condition taken from supersymmetric quantum mechanics\ \cite{Balantekin:1988aq}. Where a profile belongs to none of these families, a computable perturbative series is available instead\ \cite{Akhmedov:2008nq}.

\item \emph{Energy-batched scan.} Computed point by point, a scan over many energies at one baseline traverses the same profile once per energy, even though the profile does not depend on the energy. When $\mathbb{H}$ separates as above---which the standard, non-standard-interaction and Lorentz-violating Hamiltonians all do---we sample the potential once per refinement level and share it across every energy in the batch. The quadrature, the commutator algebra, the matrix exponentials and the slab products then carry the energy axis as a batch dimension. Energies that have already converged are masked out of further refinement rather than recomputed. One case collapses further: if $V_{\rm CC}$ does not vary along the trajectory at all, the expansion terminates at its first term. The entire scan is then a single stacked matrix exponential, exact because the expansion has no further terms left to converge.

\item \emph{Cumulative scan.} The mirror image is one energy at many baselines. Evolution operators are time-ordered products, so each requested baseline is a prefix of the next, $U(0 \to L_2) = U(L_1 \to L_2)\,U(0 \to L_1)$. Computing $N$ baselines independently therefore re-walks the profile $N$ times over. This engine walks it once, recording the running product wherever an answer was asked for. Two details are requirements rather than optimizations, both of them about memory. The traversal is chunked, so the slab operators are never all live at once. Each snapshot is converted to a probability immediately, so it collapses into the number the caller wanted instead of sitting beside it as one more complex matrix. Peak memory then scales with the chunk rather than with the number of baselines.

\item \emph{General Magnus ladder.} The terminal engine is the one described in Section \ref{sec:refinement}. It assumes nothing beyond a Hermitian $\mathbb{H}(l)$. It lays slabs along the trajectory, builds the expansion on each of them, composes them in order, and raises the slab count until two successive stages agree to the requested tolerance. Because it applies to everything, it is reached whenever nothing above it does. It is also the engine against which we check the others, since it is the only one that carries no structural assumption a test could violate, at any flavor count and on any profile.

\end{enumerate}

\textit{Seeing which engine answered.}---The fallbacks are silent by design. They happen on ordinary calls, where a warning on each would be noise. Any call can nevertheless be asked which engine answered it, whether the adiabatic strategy certified its result, and which engines stood aside and why. That is what makes an answer traceable: a result that moves when a point is added to a scan, or a call that suddenly costs more, is a change of engine, and the call reports which one. \tabl{engines} collects the condition under which each engine is taken.

\textit{Why the grouping matters more than the list.}---A cross-check between two engines is worth something only if the two can fail differently, so it matters that these six are not six independent methods. The general ladder, the cumulative scan and the energy-batched scan all walk slabs with the same kernel. They differ in how the work is batched rather than in what is computed, which makes them one family. The interaction-picture path shares that kernel but factors the fast vacuum phase out first, so the function it must resolve is a different one.

The remaining three stand apart. The adiabatic strategy is a genuinely different method, whose blind spots belong to the resonance detector rather than to the quadrature. The phase average propagates nothing at all. Finally, {\tt scipy.linalg.expm} is independent of all of them. We use it as a reference wherever it is exact---for a constant Hamiltonian, or a piecewise-constant one whose edges are declared---which is also the case the energy-batched row collapses to. Two engines in the same family can be wrong in the same way at the same time, so their disagreement is informative while their agreement is not.

\subsection{Documentation and executable examples}
\label{sec:documentation}

The main documentation is the accompanying site\ \cite{MagnusDocs}, whose source sits in the repository beside the code. \subsection{Code validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The repository carries $1250$ tests, run on Python $3.10$ through $3.13$, covering $93\%$ of the library, and including a clean-room installation of the built wheel. \ref{app:tests} groups them by what they cover. The validation is layered, its layers independent on purpose.

\smallskip

\textit{Against the derivation.}---We compare the terms $\Omega_1, \ldots, \Omega_6$ term by term against an independently coded implementation of the Bernoulli recursion, on a Hamiltonian with three independent non-commuting generators, chosen because a two-generator Hamiltonian makes one nested-commutator term of $\Omega_4$ vanish identically and would mask a coefficient error. Agreement is at machine precision for every order from one to ten, the generated terms matching the written ones.

\smallskip

\textit{Against closed forms.}---Two- and three-flavor vacuum oscillations and two-flavor constant-density matter oscillations, for neutrinos and antineutrinos, are checked against their analytic expressions to machine precision.

\smallskip

\textit{Against an independent solver.}---A tight {\tt DOP853} integration\ \cite{Prince:1981dop} at ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-12}$, ${\tt atol} = 10^{-14}$ is the accuracy oracle, and its own convergence is verified per configuration by tightening to $10^{-13}$ and confirming that the movement is far below the error being quoted. Where the profile makes {\tt scipy.linalg.expm} exact---a constant or declared piecewise-constant Hamiltonian---that is used instead, because it is not an approximation at all. The measured error ratios under slab halving are $4.0$, $16.0$ and $63.8$ at orders 2, 4 and 6 on the default Gauss--Legendre path, matching the $2^{p}$ of a global rate $p$ equal to the requested order there.

\smallskip

\textit{Against itself, where that is meaningful.}---The energy-batched scan is asserted \emph{bit-identical} to the per-point path, and a parallel run---{\tt n\_jobs} above one---bit-identical to a serial one, so an optimization that changed an answer fails rather than passes quietly. No Magnus path is ever scored against another as an accuracy measurement; a cross-check between two paths is reported as agreement and never as accuracy.

\smallskip

\textit{Against a population.}---A suite asserts on the \emph{distribution} of the error over populations of randomly generated profiles---silent-miss rate, median, worst case---rather than on individual cases, because a per-case assertion on random input is brittle while an aggregate one still catches a regression that moves the distribution. Over $145$ random smooth profiles at two through five flavors the median error is $6.1 \cdot 10^{-8}$ with $4.1\%$ silent misses; over $150$ random piecewise-constant profiles with their edges declared it is $1.3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ with none; over $164$ Earth and solar configurations, none. A \emph{silent miss} is an answer outside the requested tolerance carrying no warning of any kind. It is the only failure mode that matters: an inaccurate answer that says so is the warnings' job.

We run all of it in continuous integration on every push, across the four supported Python versions, together with a linter, a documentation build with warnings as errors, and the execution of every notebook, so that an example which stops working cannot survive a merge.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Limitations are of two kinds. Those of the method are assumptions no better programming would remove; those of the implementation are contingent on choices made in the code.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsubsection{Limitations of the method}

\textit{Open systems.}---\magnus\ solves the unitary Schr\"odinger equation for a Hermitian Hamiltonian; every truncation of the series lives in the Lie algebra, so the package is architecturally committed to norm-preserving evolution. Quantum decoherence, coupling to a bath, and neutrino decay to invisible states all remove probability from the system or damp coherence between mass eigenstates, and require a density matrix under a non-unitary master equation. A Hermitian effective Hamiltonian cannot encode a decay width---that needs an anti-Hermitian term, which breaks the very property the method rests on. {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} is the tool for those.

\smallskip

\textit{Collective oscillations.}---In a dense environment the effective Hamiltonian depends on the neutrino flavor content itself\ \cite{Duan:2010bg, Mirizzi:2015eza}, which is a nonlinear, self-consistent problem. \magnus\ assumes $\mathbb{H}$ is a \emph{known} function of energy and position supplied by the caller, not a functional of the solution.

\smallskip

\textit{A constant Hamiltonian, where a closed form is cheaper.}---Where the Hamiltonian does not change, a closed form evaluates the same operator in one step, which is arithmetic rather than a defect in anybody's code. Section \ref{sec:comparison} measures how much it is worth.

\smallskip

\textit{A feature narrower than any grid.}---Every diagnostic here samples the Hamiltonian on some grid. A feature narrower than the finest of them is invisible to all of them at once. Measured on a Gaussian resonance of width $10^{-5}$ of the trajectory, every engine reported a smooth profile, a small adiabaticity parameter, and a resolved Hamiltonian (correctly, given what any of them can see) and all were wrong together by $2.9 \cdot 10^{-2}$ against a requested $10^{-3}$. Because they are wrong \emph{together}, a cross-check between engines cannot see it either: disagreement is what a cross-check detects, so it finds a wrong engine exactly when some other engine got it right. The cure is a caller-supplied breakpoint at the feature, which takes the same case to $8.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$. This is a property of any fixed grid, not of any particular test.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsubsection{Limitations of the implementation}
\label{sec:implementation_limits}

\textit{The sterile matter potential takes one scalar per trajectory.}---The matter term factorizes as $V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}$, and $\mathbb{P}$ carries the sterile states' entry through the neutron-to-proton ratio. The density therefore varies per PREM layer while $\mathbb{P}$ does not, so on an Earth chord with a sterile state a single composition is used for a path that crosses iron and rock. No single value is right for such a chord. \magnus\ detects the case: it warns when the layer values disagree by more than $2\%$ and names the path-averaged value to pass; no number changes unless the caller acts. A per-layer projector is the fix and is tracked as an open issue; the Sun has the same shape and is worse, its composition varying more strongly with radius.

\smallskip

\textit{Broadband roughness is invisible to the structural tests.}---The sub-probe feature scan is a \emph{concentration} statistic, which is what makes it blind to structure spread evenly over every scale rather than piled into one place. We measure it on Kolmogorov density fluctuations built the way the supernova literature builds them: a $k^{-5/3}$ spectrum with a $40$--$50$~dB dynamic range, so that there is power below every grid the package lays down. On those, the finest reachable grid sees only a third of the profile's total variation. Neither structural test detects it on any of the $144$ configurations. What saves the answer there is unrelated machinery: the errors such a profile produces are caught by the \emph{convergence} checks, which watch the refinement ladder rather than the profile. The outcome is correct (the caller is warned) but by accident of mechanism. On a turbulent or noisy medium, treat the structural diagnostics as silent by construction and rely on the tolerance machinery, or supply breakpoints.

Which fluctuations hurt is not a statement about spatial features at all. Decomposed into Fourier modes, a fluctuating density couples to the flavor system through the modes whose wavenumber matches a splitting between eigenvalues of $\mathbb{H}$: those drive transitions resonantly however small their amplitude, while modes of much longer wavelength suppress the transition instead of driving it. Loreti and Balantekin first studied the effect for random fluctuations in a supernova\ \cite{Loreti:1995ae}, Patton, Kneller and McLaughlin solved the evolution through a turbulent profile and exhibited both behaviors\ \cite{Patton:2013dba, Patton:2014lza}, and the shock imprint on a burst spectrum is in \Ref\ \cite{Fogli:2006xy}. 

The practical reading is that a detector built to find narrow features in position is looking on the wrong axis. A user who knows the power spectrum of their medium should compare it against the eigenvalue splittings along the trajectory, then set {\tt n\_slabs} as a floor from the shortest wavelength carrying power near one of them, rather than expecting the ladder to discover it. That floor is honored at every refinement level, so the ladder remains free to add slabs above it while never dropping below the resolution such a medium demands of it.

\smallskip

\textit{The hybrid strategy needs a smooth Hamiltonian.}---Its diagnostics finite-difference $\mathbb{H}(l)$, which is meaningless across a genuine discontinuity, so a profile with declared breakpoints (an Earth chord, always) falls back to the general path unconditionally. That is the principled consequence of the smoothness requirement rather than a special case written in.

\smallskip

\textit{Accuracy at an engine boundary.}---Six internal engines can answer a request, listed in Section \ref{sec:engines}; the point at which one hands over to another we call a \emph{seam}. The accuracy steps across a seam rather than varying smoothly, because the engines on either side are different methods. Adding one baseline to a scan just below the seam changes the answer---by as much as five orders of magnitude in accuracy, and always \emph{toward} the truth. It is a documentation matter rather than a numerical one, but a user scanning the number of points and watching their answer move will otherwise assume something is broken.

Figure~\ref{fig:oscillogram} shows the survival probability over energy and arrival direction, for the standard case, with a sterile state, and with non-standard interactions. The Earth is the case most codes meet most often. For \magnus\ it is a mixed one: the PREM profile\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy} varies smoothly \emph{within} each of its ten shells and jumps \emph{between} them, so it exercises both halves of Section \ref{sec:refinement}. The {\tt earth} module solves the chord geometry, finds the exact positions at which a trajectory crosses each shell boundary, and hands them to the refinement as mandatory slab edges. Notebook \#6 builds oscillograms like these from scratch, and notebook \#4 treats the long-baseline case they generalize.

Not all of the structure in the standard panel is MSW resonance, and much of what appears on the core-crossing trajectories is something else. A neutrino crossing mantle, core and mantle again meets a potential that alternates rather than one that passes through a resonant value. The alternation can amplify the oscillation on its own whenever its period is commensurate with the oscillation phase---an effect found independently by Akhmedov\ \cite{Akhmedov:1988kd} and by Krastev and Smirnov\ \cite{Krastev:1989ix}, exactly solvable for an idealized periodic profile\ \cite{Akhmedov:1998ui}, and organized for the Earth by Floquet's theorem\ \cite{Akhmedov:2000js}. It amplifies a small mixing angle without needing the density to take any particular value, which is what distinguishes it from an MSW resonance. It accounts for much of the structure that makes atmospheric oscillograms sensitive to the mass ordering\ \cite{Akhmedov:2006hb, Akhmedov:2012ah}; the sensitivity is carried by these very trajectories rather than by the horizon, where the chord is short, the matter effect weakest, and the pattern close to the vacuum one. 

In the sterile panels the sharp feature at high energy is a genuine resonance whose origin is the reverse of the usual one. A sterile state feels no matter potential at all, so what drives it is the neutral-current potential that the active states do feel and it does not. The crossing sits near the energy at which $\Delta m^2_{41}$ balances that potential, which locates the feature to within tens of percent---close enough to identify the feature, not close enough to replace the calculation that produced the panel.

One thing the figure does not carry is the spread in baseline. We take the path length from the arrival direction alone, whereas a real atmospheric neutrino is produced somewhere in a column of atmosphere tens of kilometers deep, a column the geometry stretches as the trajectory flattens---from a few tens of kilometers overhead to a few hundred near the horizon\ \cite{Gaisser:1997eu}. Folding in that distribution is an averaging step above the probability rather than inside it. A code that takes the baseline from the zenith angle alone is making it silently.

One convention goes with it, and the caller can override it. PREM is a density model and fixes no composition, so the electron fraction is supplied separately. \magnus\ resolves it per PREM layer (about $0.4656$ for the iron core and $0.4957$ for the mantle) which differs from a uniform $0.5$ by up to $7 \cdot 10^{-2}$ in $P_{\nu_\mu \to \nu_e}$ on a core-crossing chord. That is far above anything a solver comparison can resolve, which is why every cross-code measurement in Section \ref{sec:comparison} passes the composition explicitly on both sides rather than leaving it to a default.

The lowest panel measures the departure from adiabaticity, and it is worth being clear that this is a physical quantity rather than an error. Its reference is the adiabatic limit assembled from the instantaneous eigenbases alone: diagonalize $\mathbb{H}$ at production and at the surface, then contract the two sets of eigenvector magnitudes. That touches none of the machinery of Section \ref{sec:averaged}, so it serves all four scenarios rather than only the standard one, where it reduces to the textbook expression. All four agree with it to between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-8}$ across $0.1$--$20$~MeV, which brackets the solar spectrum.

Computing all three flavors also lets us price the shortcut that most solar analyses take. The standard reduction decouples the third state up to its admixture and solves a two-flavor problem at an electron density rescaled by $\cos^2\theta_{13}$\ \cite{Kuo:1989qe}, giving $P^{3\nu}_{\nu_e \to \nu_e} = \sin^4\theta_{13} + \cos^4\theta_{13}\,P^{2\nu}_{\nu_e \to \nu_e}$. It is exact in vacuum but not in matter, where what it drops is more often quoted than measured. Running both sides on the same profile and the same parameters, we find the reduction low by $1.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$ in the median across $0.1$--$20$~MeV, rising monotonically with energy to $1.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ at $20$~MeV, and holding a median of $8.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ above $5$~MeV. The next-to-leading correction that removes most of this has been derived\ \cite{Lim:2002iz} and is not in general use. The discrepancy is far above what \magnus\ costs to avoid it. It is also a modeling error rather than a numerical one: no tolerance reaches it, because both sides are converged.

\subsubsection{Averaging a scan is not asking for the average}
\label{sec:averaging_is_not_estimating}

A scan can also be averaged after the fact: compute the instantaneous probability across a window in energy and take its mean. That estimator does not converge.

A finite-window mean is an estimator with a bias of its own, and on a profile whose density varies across the window that bias does not shrink as the window widens---because a wider window also averages over different matter conditions. Measured on the solar ray above, the window mean moves from $0.592$ to $0.602$ between six and forty-eight oscillation lengths, drifting \emph{away} from rather than toward a limit. There is no width at which one can stop and claim convergence. Asked for the limit instead, and run at two flavors on a smooth exponential profile so that the closed form applies, \magnus\ reproduces the analytic adiabatic MSW expression $\langle P_{ee}\rangle = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\theta_m(l_0)\cos2\theta_m(l_1)$ to $3 \cdot 10^{-16}$ across $1$--$20$~MeV, and matches it to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-5}$ on the full tabulated model where the analytic expression is itself an idealization. Forty averaged energies take about $0.7$~s.


The obvious guard does not catch the related trap. \magnus\ reports, on request, how coarsely a scan samples the oscillation it is computing---the number of cycles per step, and how many points a Nyquist-adequate scan would need. On the trajectories this package serves those numbers are stark: an Earth chord is about $430$ oscillations long and would need $861$ baselines, a solar ray about $2200$ and $4390$, a supernova ray about $37\,000$ and $73\,392$. The measurement is \emph{reported and never warned about}, deliberately: the Nyquist criterion is objectively correct and would fire on $44$ of $45$ realistic scan sizes, and a warning firing on $98\%$ of calls is noise however right each firing is---and it would teach users to silence a category that also carries genuine discontinuity warnings.

At a front width of $0.07$~km each jump is, on the scale of the oscillation length, a discontinuity: the probability changes character across a single point. Widened to $70$~km, nothing else altered, the same jump becomes a smooth function spanning half the window, across which the transition is gradual. We widen both jumps together, which is why the two columns already differ below the forward shock and not only at it. 

The oscillation length that matters here is the local one. The relevant pair here is the solar one, so the length is $4\pi E/\Delta m^2_{21}$ rather than the $\lambda_{\rm max} - \lambda_{\rm min}$ of \equ{phase}: in vacuum at $15$~MeV that is some $500$~km, which makes the ray $140$ of them long, while at the density of the contact discontinuity the matter potential shortens it to $16$~km. The fastest pair oscillates some thirty times more quickly, which is what the panels alias. A $0.07$~km ramp is therefore crossed faster than the state can respond, which projects it onto the new eigenbasis; a $70$~km one is crossed slowly enough that the state follows the instantaneous eigenstate instead. What differs between the columns is therefore the conversion probability itself rather than the phase of an oscillation, which the two columns share.

The wider contact ramp begins at $12\,313$~km. Past it the two columns differ by $0.22$ on average through the shocked region, the wider front leaving a mean survival of $0.91$ against $0.72$. Sweeping the width confirms both halves of that statement: the columns stay together for every ramp thinner than the local oscillation length, and part once it is reached. The width at which they part follows that length as we vary the energy, at $6.3$~km for $5$~MeV, $15.7$~km for $15$~MeV and $46.6$~km for $45$~MeV. Those are the local oscillation lengths themselves, which grow linearly with the energy, and reproducing them needs nothing but $4\pi E/\Delta m^2_{21}$ evaluated at the density of the contact. It is the signature of a change in adiabaticity rather than of a resolution effect, whose width would not track the energy.

Away from the two transitions the profiles are identical to the last bit, so nothing else in the figure can be responsible. The four scenarios drawn differ only in the Hermitian matrix handed to the same call. Two flavors barely oscillate here, since only $\Delta m^2_{21}$ is available to them at this energy; the others convert strongly, and the sterile system most of all.

The question to ask of a profile is not what physics is in it but what it looks like at the scale of the oscillation length. A staircase belongs to a closed form and a smooth function to an integrator. The profile decides, and the same physical object can be either, depending on how well it is resolved. Section \ref{sec:shock_comparison} puts numbers to both halves of that.

\subsection{High-energy astrophysical neutrinos}
\label{sec:astro}

Figure~\ref{fig:astro} shows the flavor composition that a TeV--PeV astrophysical flux arrives with at Earth, and the average flavor-transition probabilities behind it, for five Hamiltonians over $1$~TeV to $10$~PeV. The top row carries the survival probabilities $\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha}\rangle$; the bottom row carries the flavor fractions reaching Earth from a source that produces neutrinos through the full pion decay chain. The standard three-flavor case is repeated beneath each panel so that a departure from it is legible, and the dotted line in the second column marks the energy at which the Lorentz-violating term equals the vacuum one.

\textit{Producing them.}---In cosmic accelerators---active galaxies, gamma-ray bursts, supernovae---protons interact with ambient matter and radiation to produce pions\ \cite{Margolis:1977wt, Stecker:1978ah, Kelner:2006tc}. The pions decay through $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + \nu_\mu$, followed by $\mu^+ \to \bar{\nu}_\mu + \nu_e + e^+$, and their charge conjugates, so that the flux leaving the source carries flavor ratios $(f_e, f_\mu, f_\tau)_{\rm S} = \left(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, 0\right)$, with $f_{\alpha, {\rm S}}$ the fraction of $\nu_\alpha + \bar{\nu}_\alpha$ in the total. Neutrino telescopes cannot ordinarily tell $\nu$ from $\bar\nu$, so $\nu_\alpha$ below means $\nu_\alpha + \bar{\nu}_\alpha$. We take that full pion-decay composition throughout. Notebook \#10 develops the averaged limit that this section rests on, and notebook \#23 says when averaging a scan is not the same as asking for the average.

\textit{What is observable.}---Over Mpc--Gpc baselines the oscillation length is minute against the distance, and neither the distance, the size of the production region, nor the energy resolution of a detector is known to anything approaching the precision the phase would demand. A telescope is therefore sensitive only to the average flavor-transition probabilities $\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}\rangle$ of \equ{averaged}, which \magnus\ returns in closed form from the eigenvectors of $\mathbb{H}$ rather than by propagating a phase it is about to average away. In vacuum $\mathbb{V}$ is the PMNS matrix and \equ{averaged} is the familiar $\sum_i \lvert U_{\alpha i}\rvert^2 \lvert U_{\beta i}\rvert^2$; for a sterile state or a new term in the \emph{vacuum} Hamiltonian it is the same expression evaluated in whatever eigenbasis that Hamiltonian has. Matter is different, and the difference matters here: the flux decoheres in the vacuum mass basis long before it arrives, and then crosses the Earth \emph{coherently}. The two legs therefore compose as probability matrices rather than as amplitudes, $f_{\beta,\oplus} = \sum_{\alpha,\gamma} f_\alpha \langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\gamma}\rangle P^{\oplus}_{\nu_\gamma \to \nu_\beta}$, with the second factor an ordinary propagation through the profile. Evaluating \equ{averaged} in the eigenbasis of the Earth Hamiltonian instead would be wrong, and badly so: above a TeV the matter term dominates, its eigenvectors are nearly the flavor states, and the expression would return the source composition unchanged. The composition at Earth follows as $f_{\alpha, \oplus} = \sum_\beta \langle P_{\nu_\beta \to \nu_\alpha}\rangle f_{\beta, {\rm S}}$. Listing \ref{lst:astro} is both steps. Oscillation over astrophysical distances populates a $\nu_\tau$ component that the sources do not make, which is what turns the composition at Earth into an observable and, run backwards, into a constraint on the production mechanism\ \cite{Learned:1994wg, Athar:2000yw, Beacom:2003nh, Farzan:2008eg, Song:2020nfh}.

Nothing here is specific to the standard case: pass a Hamiltonian carrying a sterile state or a new term and the same two lines answer. {\tt H} is any energy-independent vacuum Hamiltonian from {\tt magnus.hamiltonians}, divided by the energy; notebook \#28 builds the four drawn in \figu{astro}.}, label={lst:astro}, float=tbp]

\textit{What varies with energy, and what does not.}---For standard oscillations the composition does not depend on the energy at all. Equation~(\ref{equ:averaged}) is built from $\lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}\rvert^2$ alone, and in vacuum no energy enters it. Two of the three departures we draw are flat for the same reason. A sterile state moves the composition from $0.326:0.343:0.330$ to $0.330:0.325:0.346$ and leaves it there, after we renormalize past the $13.3\%$ of the flux it removes. Non-standard interactions through the Earth move it by less than $0.005$ and likewise do not vary, because above a TeV the matter term dominates the Hamiltonian inside the Earth and carries no energy dependence of its own. Both are shifts to a different constant, flat across the whole decade drawn. The Earth panel is drawn over that decade to show the machinery, not to predict a rate: above about $100$~TeV a core-crossing chord is opaque, and the attenuation that dominates is an open-system effect outside a unitary solver (Section \ref{sec:limitations}).

What separates the fourth case is that its term keeps growing relative to the others across the decade drawn, so the eigenvectors never settle; the other three reach a regime in which one term dominates and the composition stops moving. The Lorentz-violating case carries $\nu_e$ from $0.326$ up to $0.348$ and down again to $0.317$ across the crossover, with $\nu_\mu$ dipping to $0.328$ and recovering; the survival probability driving it reaches $0.894$. That is what a flavor measurement across a decade of energy could distinguish, and it requires evaluating \equ{averaged} for a Hamiltonian nobody has written down.

The fifth case is where \equ{averaged_blocks} is needed, though the figure itself does not exercise the block sum: at $10^{-13}$~eV$^2$ over $100$~Mpc every pair has long decohered, so the grouping finds six singletons and \equ{averaged_blocks} reduces to \equ{averaged} on a six-state spectrum. Notebook \#29 sweeps the splitting through the coherent and intermediate regimes where the blocks are genuinely formed. If neutrinos are pseudo-Dirac\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1981kw, Petcov:1982ya}, a mass state is really two, separated by a splitting far below anything a terrestrial experiment resolves; the pair then stays mutually coherent long after the three standard splittings have averaged away. Which expression applies is then a question about the spectrum rather than about the code: at $100$~Mpc and $100$~TeV a pair contributes coherently below about $2 \cdot 10^{-19}$~eV$^2$, where \equ{averaged_blocks} returns the unpaired answer exactly, and has decohered above about $10^{-16}$~eV$^2$, where \equ{averaged} counts its two members separately and halves the channels they carry.

\magnus\ groups the spectrum itself rather than being told which case it is in, and refuses to average at all in the band between, where neither expression describes the physics. We pair the second mass state alone, a four-state spectrum, which is the case the interface exists for: pairing all three would suppress every channel by the same factor and leave the fractions where they were, so only an uneven pairing moves the observable. The signature it produces at a neutrino telescope is the one identified in \Ref\ \cite{Beacom:2003eu}. Notebook \#29 develops the model, sweeps a splitting through all three regimes, and shows that the same spectrum is invisible at a terrestrial baseline.

\subsection{A Hamiltonian of your own}
\label{sec:arbitrary}

Everything above goes through named wrappers. Each assembles a Hamiltonian and hands it to {\tt osc\_prob}; a caller who already has one can skip that first step. Listing \ref{lst:arbitrary} does exactly that, passing a Hamiltonian the package knows nothing about, which is the escape hatch and also the design.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={\magnus\ with an arbitrary, user-supplied Hamiltonian, here the long-range $L_e - L_\mu$ interaction of \equ{h3_lri} sourced by the electrons of the Sun. The profile and $V_{\rm CC}$ are the solar model of \figu{solar}; only $V_{e\mu}$ is new, and what it costs the caller is three lines---the potential, the charge matrix $\mathbb{Q}$, and the extra term inside {\tt H\_func}. The {\tt [..., None, None]} is what lets one call evaluate the Hamiltonian at every quadrature node of every slab at once; written to take a scalar, the same function returns bit-identical numbers $4.6$ times slower, one call per node instead of one per slab. {\tt long\_range\_potential}, {\tt vcc\_sun} and the solar grids are built in notebook \#19, which derives the potential and checks it against a closed form; this listing is the last two cells of it.}, label={lst:arbitrary}, float=tbp]

import magnus.avgprob as avgprob
import magnus.globaldefs as gd
import magnus.hamiltonians as hamiltonians

# Energy-independent; H_func divides by E below
h_vac = hamiltonians.\
 hamiltonian_3nu_vacuum_energy_independent(**osc)
# V_CC reaches nu_e only
e00 = np.diag([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])

# V_emu [eV] on the solar radial grid, from the
# Yukawa integral of Eq. (21) over the same n_e(r)
# the matter term reads, so the two potentials
# cannot disagree about the electron distribution
v_emu = g2*long_range_potential(r, n_e, 1.0/R_SUN)

# nu_e and nu_mu carry opposite charge, nu_tau none
q = np.diag([1.0, -1.0, 0.0])

def H_func(l):
 VCC = np.asarray(vcc_sun(l))
 return (h_vac/energy
 + VCC[..., None, None]*e00
 + np.interp(l, r, v_emu)[..., None, None]*q)

\end{lstlisting}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Two properties govern how one should be written. The first is that the engine samples the Hamiltonian at every quadrature node of every slab (a few hundred positions for a single probability, repeated at each refinement level) so a function that accepts only one position at a time turns that into a Python loop. \magnus\ probes the function once, verifies a vectorized evaluation against a scalar spot-check, and uses whichever form works; the fallback is correct and $4.6$ times slower, with bit-identical output, and since v1.0.0 it says so once per session and names the fix. The {\tt [..., None, None]} in Listing \ref{lst:arbitrary} is the whole of that fix. The second is that a Hamiltonian which ignores its argument is detected separately and routed to the constant-Hamiltonian engine, so the easiest case there is does not take the slowest path available. It used to: the dispatcher declined a non-callable potential, so a $60$-energy constant-density scan made $18\,000$ separate calls, each rediscovering that constancy.

Figure~\ref{fig:solar_lri} puts one such Hamiltonian to work, on the solar ray of Section \ref{sec:sun}. \emph{Top}: the phase-averaged survival probability $\langle P_{\nu_e \to \nu_e}\rangle$ against energy, for the standard three-flavor Hamiltonian and for two versions of a new one, at mediator ranges of $R_\odot$ and a tenth of it. \emph{Bottom}: the difference each makes. Nothing in the package anticipates the new term, and nothing needs to: the same averaging machinery of Section \ref{sec:averaged} answers, because it never asked what the Hamiltonian contained.

\textit{The interaction.}---The example is a long-range neutrino-electron interaction, made possible by a new light neutral gauge boson $Z^\prime$ from gauging the anomaly-free combination $L_e - L_\mu$\ \cite{He:1990pn, Foot:1994vd}, with $L_e$ and $L_\mu$ the electron and muon lepton numbers. The charge under it, $Q \equiv L_e - L_\mu$, is $+1$ for the electron and $\nu_e$, $-1$ for the muon and $\nu_\mu$, and zero for every other Standard-Model field. A new matter potential sourced by electrons therefore enters the Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_lri}
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm LRI}(E, l)
 =
 \frac{\mathbb{A}}{E} + V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}
 + V_{e\mu}(l)\,{\rm diag}\left(1, -1, 0\right) \;,
\end{equation}
where the first two terms are the vacuum and charged-current matter terms of Section \ref{sec:recap} and the third is the whole of the modification, supplied by the caller as one more additive matrix.

\textit{A non-local potential.}---Under $L_e - L_\mu$ an electron sources a Yukawa potential centered on its position, so what a neutrino at $\mathbf{r}$ feels is an integral over the electrons around it,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:yukawa}
 V_{e\mu}(\mathbf{r})
 =
 \frac{g^{\prime 2}}{4\pi}
 \int d^3r^\prime \, n_e(\mathbf{r}^\prime) \,
 \frac{e^{-m_{Z^\prime} \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}^\prime \rvert}}
      {\lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}^\prime \rvert} \;,
\end{equation}
with $g^\prime$ the new gauge coupling. Where $V_{\rm CC}$ reads the density at the neutrino's own position, \equ{yukawa} is \emph{non-local}: at mediator masses light enough to matter its value at any radius is an integral over the whole electron distribution, so it depends on how many electrons there are and only weakly on where they sit\ \cite{Wise:2018rnb}. Its variation along a trajectory is a matter of geometry rather than of the local density.

That is what makes the Sun a sharper test of it than the Earth. The solar density falls by five orders of magnitude along the ray, so $V_{\rm CC}$ and $V_{e\mu}$ fall at quite different rates, and the shape of the new potential---not merely its size---is what the observable responds to. We fix the coupling separately for each mediator range so that both put $V_{e\mu}$ at a tenth of $V_{\rm CC}$ at the center; what then differs between the two curves is that shape alone. At half a solar radius the longer-ranged potential still stands at $2.5$ times $V_{\rm CC}$ where the shorter has fallen to $0.4$.

The two shift the averaged probability by up to $0.021$ and $0.018$ across $0.1$--$20$~MeV, and they are not a rescaling of one another: the shorter range samples the profile where it falls fastest, and its shift changes sign at a different energy. Both curves are computed with no expansion in $V_{e\mu}$, because the method does not care what the Hermitian matrix contains. Notebook \#19 develops the potential and checks it against a closed form where one exists.

\section{Performance and precision}
\label{sec:performance}

We measured every timing quoted in this paper on one machine, so the absolute numbers carry little on their own; the \emph{ratios} are what transfer. Where a measurement compares two versions or two code paths, we take it with a harness that interleaves them round-robin and carries workloads the change cannot touch as controls. Those controls came back at exactly $1.00\times$ even while an unrelated process was saturating one of the machine's cores, which is what makes the ratios usable. A factor of two or more between two codes is a property of the codes and survives a change of machine; a margin of a few percent belongs to the machine rather than to either code. A tolerance sweep is read within one code's own curve for the same reason.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{What a probability costs}
\label{sec:cost}

A single three-flavor Earth probability takes about $2$~ms at the default tolerance. Across $164$ Earth and solar configurations spanning two to five flavors, standard and non-standard Hamiltonians, neutrinos and antineutrinos, the median call is $2$~ms and the slowest is $0.90$~s. Three things make a scan much faster than its parts, none of them changing any answer.

\smallskip

\textit{Pass arrays.}---Every wrapper takes an array of energies, of baselines, or both. For a position-dependent Hamiltonian we then build the profile once for the whole scan rather than once per point (what the energy-batched engine exists for). Where the potential does not vary at all, the constant-Hamiltonian engine turns the scan into one stacked exponential. Measured against the per-point route it replaces, that is $15.5$--$18.9\times$ at three flavors, $17.3$--$24.7\times$ at two, and $6$--$7.4\times$ at four and five. The last two gain less because they exponentiate through {\tt eigh}. Results are bit-identical to the route they replace at every flavor count and both neutrino signs.

\smallskip

\textit{Make the Hamiltonian array-capable.}---The single largest factor under a caller's control, measured at $4.6\times$ on a three-flavor exponential-density profile with bit-identical output.

\smallskip

\textit{An Earth chord is a palindrome.}---A neutrino crossing a spherically symmetric Earth meets every radius twice, so we evaluate the Hamiltonian on the first half of the slab chain and take the rest by reversal. That halves the calls to the caller's Hamiltonian and nothing else; we leave the exponential and the commutator algebra untouched. It is worth exactly what that Hamiltonian costs: $0.91\times$ for a plain PREM density lookup, too cheap to be worth halving, and $1.41$--$1.67\times$ for an expensive one. We declare the symmetry rather than detect it, because detecting it would need the very evaluations the optimization exists to skip.

The Earth entry points are where that declaration belongs: there, a chord meeting every radius twice is a fact of geometry rather than an assumption about the profile. The symmetry is carried by the interval it applies to rather than set as a global flag. A standard PREM energy scan gains nothing from it, because the separable engine already evaluates the profile once and shares it across every energy---the same saving taken earlier and more completely.

\smallskip

One cost runs the other way. The adaptive ladder computes the probability at several slab counts and stops when two agree, so a call at a tight tolerance pays for the levels below it as well as for the confidence it buys. Passing a slab count directly skips that, at the cost of the evidence it buys.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Precision, accuracy, and diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

Precision and accuracy have separate limits here. Precision is internal. The batched and per-point paths, and the parallel and serial ones, agree bit-for-bit, which the test suite asserts as an identity rather than a tolerance. The two matrix-exponential backends agree to about $10^{-15}$, and to $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ on a solar profile that chains $33\,575$ slab exponentials---within the $N\varepsilon = 7.4 \cdot 10^{-12}$ that an ordered product of that length allows, so the two backends are indistinguishable at that depth.

Accuracy is measured against something outside the library. Section \ref{sec:validation} gives the oracles and the distributions. At the default {\tt rtol} $=$ {\tt atol} $= 10^{-3}$ an Earth crossing comes back accurate to about $5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ against a $10^{-7}$-tolerance reference.

In a binned atmospheric analysis that accuracy is not the binding constraint. The probability is evaluated on a grid in energy and arrival direction and interpolated between the nodes, then folded with a flux, a cross-section and a detector response. The error introduced by that grid and its interpolant is orders of magnitude larger than anything the engine beneath contributes\ \cite{IceCube:2018ikn}. A code accurate to $10^{-13}$ and one accurate to $10^{-6}$ are indistinguishable once both have passed through that chain. 

What the extra digits do buy is everything upstream of it: a reference against which another code, or a coarser setting of this one, can be checked; a regime where no closed form exists and there is nothing else to compare against; an observable that is computed once rather than binned, as in Sections \ref{sec:sun} and \ref{sec:astro}; and the ability to separate a method's error from a model's, which is what Section \ref{sec:comparison} does throughout. We report the reach because it makes those uses possible, not because a fit needs it.

The tolerance is a stopping criterion rather than an error bound, so a user leans on the warnings instead. Measured across the profile families the package serves, they carry high false-positive rates by design: most report a condition about the input rather than a prediction about the error. On the hardest of those families, about one answer in twenty-five came back wrong with no warning at all.

\section{Comparison to other codes}
\label{sec:comparison}

This section measures \magnus\ against {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} throughout, and against {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, Arguelles:2021twb, NuSQuIDS} in Section \ref{sec:solar_comparison} alone, where the observable is the phase-averaged solar probability. We referee every case by a method that is neither code's; in Sections \ref{sec:const_density} to \ref{sec:solar_comparison} we time every code in one process on one machine, while the cross-code planes of Section \ref{sec:prem_planes} carry external timings measured by the companion project on the same machine model in a separate session, as their caption records. The frozen datasets behind the figures are in the repository so that none of the external codes need be installed to redraw them.

\textbf{Where a closed form exists and the accumulated phase is large, use the closed form.} Constant density, piecewise-constant PREM, and standard three-flavor propagation are exactly what closed forms are built for, and on those \magnus\ does not win on cost. What it buys is everything that is not that: accuracy past the point where a piecewise-constant discretization stalls, an arbitrary varying profile, a Hamiltonian nobody has diagonalized, five flavors, and observables we return rather than reconstruct from a scan of instantaneous probabilities.

We measure it in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}: on a piecewise-constant PREM chord {\tt NuOscProbExact} is about $20$ times cheaper than \magnus\ at three flavors, and about $400$ times at $3+1$. What \magnus\ buys is what lies outside it: accuracy past the point where a piecewise-constant discretization stalls, an arbitrary varying profile, a Hamiltonian nobody has diagonalized, five flavors, and observables we return rather than reconstruct from a scan of instantaneous probabilities.

\subsection{Relation to other oscillation codes}
\label{sec:relation}

Several mature codes compute oscillation probabilities. They overlap less than their common subject suggests. {\tt GLoBES}\ \cite{GLoBES, Huber:2007ji} is an experiment-simulation framework in which probabilities are one layer inside a package built for fluxes, cross-sections and detector response. {\tt Prob3++}\ \cite{Prob3pp} evaluates the analytic three-flavor probability in matter\ \cite{Barger:1980tf} and is long established in atmospheric analyses. {\tt nuCraft}\ \cite{nuCraft} integrates numerically along a chord through the Earth. {\tt NuFast-LBL}\ \cite{Denton:2024pzc} and {\tt NuFast-Earth}\ \cite{Denton:2025oxf} are fast specializations of the three-flavor case. {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} evaluates closed-form SU($n$) expansions of the evolution operator for an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, and four flavors, and composes them across slabs of constant density. {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, Arguelles:2021twb, NuSQuIDS} is the most general: it propagates a density matrix rather than a state, which admits interactions, attenuation and open-system terms that no closed form reaches, at the cost of solving a differential equation rather than composing exponentials.

Several others serve particular sectors: {\tt OscProb}\ \cite{OscProb} and {\tt CUDAProb3}\ \cite{Kallenborn:2019ilo} evaluate layered Earth profiles for atmospheric analyses, the latter on graphics hardware; {\tt Neurthino.jl}\ \cite{Neurthino} and {\tt NuProbe}\ \cite{Fong:2022oim} extend beyond three flavors; {\tt Nu-Pert}\ \cite{NuPert} expands about a constant density; and {\tt PEANUTS}\ \cite{PEANUTS} computes the solar flux and its composition on an adiabatic treatment, which makes it the natural comparison for the averaged solar observable of Section \ref{sec:sun}. Three of these codes now return derivatives of the probability---analytically in {\tt CHIC}\ \cite{Fernandez-Menendez:2025qbi}, by automatic differentiation in {\tt MANGO}\ \cite{Granger:2026qdr} and {\tt nuTens}\ \cite{nuTens}---which \magnus\ does not, and which a user fitting parameters will want to know before adopting it.

\magnus\ sits between the last two. The difference from each is structural rather than a matter of degree. Against a closed-form slab code, the difference is what happens \emph{inside} a slab: {\tt NuOscProbExact} freezes the Hamiltonian there and is exact for the frozen one, while \magnus\ integrates the Hamiltonian across the slab and is exact to the order of its truncation for the real one. Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product} shows that the two coincide exactly at second order---a piecewise-constant composition with the density read at each slab's midpoint \emph{is} the order-2 Magnus method---so what we add on the same slab chain is the higher orders, and the flavor counts and Hamiltonians that a closed form cannot express. Against {\tt nuSQuIDS}, the difference is that a unitary state-vector method cannot represent an open system at all, and pays nothing for the generality it does not have.

\subsection{Constant density}
\label{sec:const_density}

Both codes are exact here, to round-off. Over $60$ energies from $0.6$ to $20$~GeV at $1300$~km through $2.848$~g~cm$^{-3}$, \magnus\ reaches $9.0 \cdot 10^{-17}$ and {\tt NuOscProbExact} $4.9 \cdot 10^{-16}$, both at round-off; batched, \magnus\ costs $9.7~\mu$s per point and {\tt NuOscProbExact} $1.3~\mu$s. Called one energy at a time, \emph{either} code costs about an order of magnitude more per probability---$95~\mu$s and $14~\mu$s respectively. The dominant effect on this problem is how the code is called, not which code it is.

\smallskip

\textit{Cost: the closed form, decisively.}---On a chord at $\cos\theta_z = -0.85$ at three flavors, {\tt NuOscProbExact} is cheaper throughout, but by how much depends on what is asked of each code. Against its tightest row in \tabl{prem}, $59~\mu$s for an error of $5.6 \cdot 10^{-5}$, \magnus\ costs $153~\mu$s and delivers $5.4 \cdot 10^{-6}$: a factor of $2.6$ in cost for ten times the accuracy. Comparing the two codes' tightest rows instead gives $59~\mu$s against $1\,254$, a factor of $21$---but those settings differ by five orders of magnitude in error, and no slabbing of the closed form reaches \magnus's accuracy on this chord, so that ratio prices reach rather than speed. At $3+1$ the gap is larger and of a different kind: $280~\mu$s against $34\,000$, a factor of about $120$, and the cost does not fall when the tolerance is loosened. That flatness is itself the diagnosis: the refinement ladder is not converging and stopping, it is running to its slab ceiling, because an eV-scale $\Delta m^2_{41}$ over an $11\,000$~km chord demands a slab width below what the ladder reaches. \magnus\ warns on every row.

\smallskip

\textit{Reach: the expansion, decisively.}---Refined against its own tightest setting, \magnus\ reaches $3.3 \cdot 10^{-10}$ at three flavors where {\tt NuOscProbExact} stalls near $5.6 \cdot 10^{-5}$ at its densest slabbing. The residual between the two codes is $4.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$, the same order as the \emph{looser} of the two curves, so most of it is the closed form's discretization rather than a disagreement about physics. A residual far above \emph{both} curves would have meant a convention mismatch instead, which is the check we make before concluding anything from a cross-code difference.

At $3+1$ the accuracy runs the same way, with a sharper diagnosis. \magnus's residual against the referee is $4.5 \cdot 10^{-8}$, \emph{below} the referee's own discretization error of $4.1 \cdot 10^{-7}$, so the honest statement is that it agrees with the referee to within the referee's uncertainty and this comparison cannot resolve it further. {\tt NuOscProbExact} sits at $2.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$, some six hundred times above that floor. Its convergence is \emph{not monotonic}: $32$ slabs per segment is worse than $8$. Non-monotonic convergence is the signature of a discretization whose edges straddle structure they do not resolve. There is no setting of that dial to read off as converged, because the error does not fall as it is turned.

\smallskip

The two findings sit together uncomfortably. \magnus\ warns on every $3+1$ row (correctly, since the answer is not backed by a convergence argument) and is also the most accurate answer on the plot. \textbf{A convergence warning is a statement about evidence, not about error}. This is exactly where the two come apart: the warning is correct and the number is accurate at the same time, which no single figure of merit expresses.

\subsection{A smooth profile: where each method runs out}
\label{sec:smooth_comparison}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure*}[t!]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{smooth_reach.pdf}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure~\ref{fig:smooth_reach} shows what each method costs and how far each can be pushed, on a smooth exponential profile and on an Earth chord, at two through five flavors. Here the comparison stops being about cost and becomes about reach: there is a floor and there is a ceiling.

The floor is the slab product's. Composing $N_{\rm slabs}$ constant-density operators accumulates round-off in the product itself; past about $16\,000$ slabs that accumulation costs more than another halving of the width buys. At three flavors a request for $10^{-10}$ therefore cannot be met at any slab count: the refinement stalls near $2.7 \cdot 10^{-11}$; the curve ends there rather than reaching the last tolerance. This is not a defect in {\tt NuOscProbExact}, but a property of any long ordered product evaluated in floating point; \magnus\ meets the same wall. It meets it two decades further down at order 4, nearly three at order 8, because a higher-order rule reaches a given accuracy in far fewer factors than a second-order one does.

The ceiling is the flavor count, which is why the bottom row carries no closed form at all: there is no five-flavor SU($n$) expression to draw.

The closed form is the faster code at loose tolerances; where it stops being so depends on the profile and on the flavor count. Compared at the accuracy each code actually reaches, rather than at the tolerance each was asked for, \magnus\ becomes the cheaper route below $7.5 \cdot 10^{-9}$ on the exponential profile at three flavors, but below $1.9 \cdot 10^{-5}$ on the Earth chord: more than three decades earlier, on the profile whose layers give the quadrature something to resolve. It is not that the closed form cuts the trajectory more coarsely; it cuts it far more finely. On the Earth chord at two flavors we measure $380$ slabs for \magnus\ at a requested $10^{-8}$, against $139\,264$ for {\tt NuOscProbExact}, a factor of $366$. The slab is not the unit of cost. We fit time against slab count over the two tightest dials and find $0.20~\mu$s per \magnus\ slab at order 4, against $0.054~\mu$s for a closed-form slab (the tightest dials separate the fixed cost best, since it is amortized over the most slabs there). A factor of four is small for what a slab carries: order 4 samples $\mathbb{A}$ at two Gauss-Legendre nodes, forms a commutator, assembles $\Omega$, then exponentiates a general matrix, where the closed form evaluates one SU(2) expression at one density. What makes the heavier slab nearly as cheap is that none of it runs in the interpreter. At these matrix sizes---$2 \times 2$ here, $5 \times 5$ at most---the arithmetic in a slab is a few hundred floating-point operations, so the bookkeeping around the arithmetic can cost more than the arithmetic itself: a {\tt NumPy} expression evaluates each operation across every slab before starting the next, writing every intermediate to memory as a full array, while a Python loop over slabs pays interpreter dispatch once per slab. \magnus\ takes neither route. Compiled kernels finish one slab before moving to the next---the quadrature combinations that build $\Omega$ at orders 4, 6, and 8, the commutators inside them, the check that $\Omega$ is anti-Hermitian, the exponential of Section \ref{sec:exponential}, and the running product that composes the slabs---so a slab costs what its arithmetic costs. The kernels change the clock rather than the mathematics: each reproduces the expression it replaces bit for bit or to within the last-bit rounding of a reordered matrix product (shifts at the $10^{-14}$ level, every refinement decision unchanged); the exception is the eigensolver behind the four- and five-flavor exponential, to which we return below. What \magnus\ still carries that the closed form does not is a fixed cost of about $140~\mu$s, its refinement paying for the chains it evaluates and discards; at a requested $10^{-3}$ on this chord that alone exceeds the closed form's whole bill, which is why the loose end of every panel still belongs to the staircase. The crossover falls where the closed form's slab bill outgrows that fixed cost, and past it \magnus\ pulls away: five times faster at $10^{-6}$, thirty-four times at $10^{-8}$.

That factor is not a constant, which is why the flavor count moves the crossover too. A closed-form slab costs $0.054$, $0.146$ and $1.48~\mu$s at two, three and four flavors on this chord: the SU(4) expression is nearly thirty times the SU(2) one, where \magnus\ exchanges only a $2 \times 2$ exponential for a $4 \times 4$. Three flavors is where the imbalance helps most, moving the crossover to $1.9 \cdot 10^{-5}$ from $2.5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ at two. At $3+1$ there is no crossover left to quote, because the two curves no longer bracket each other. \magnus\ there runs to its slab ceiling instead of converging---a requested $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ return the same $1.5 \cdot 10^{-7}$---so the coarse end of that panel belongs to the closed form alone, errors of a few times $10^{-5}$ in well under a millisecond being a setting \magnus\ cannot even be dialed onto; at every accuracy both codes deliver, \magnus\ is the cheaper route, several times cheaper at $10^{-6}$ and nearly twenty times at $10^{-8}$. Part of the reason is the exponential itself. At four and five flavors it goes through a batched Jacobi eigensolver, warm-started from one slab to the next, rather than through LAPACK's {\tt eigh}, whose fixed per-matrix overhead on a $4 \times 4$ was two thirds of a whole pass; the price of trading one iterative eigensolver for another is visible at the very bottom of the bottom row, where the tightest smooth-profile points land up to a factor of three higher than {\tt eigh} put them ($3.3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ against $1.0 \cdot 10^{-12}$ at five flavors), with every looser point unchanged.

That crossover moves with the cost of the Hamiltonian. \figu{smooth_reach} measures the case least favorable to \magnus: a PREM density read, about as cheap as a Hamiltonian can be. \magnus\ asks for $\mathbb{A}$ at two Gauss-Legendre nodes per slab, the closed form at one density per slab, so the sample counts follow the slab counts rather than the times. Counting every sample each code requests for one twelve-energy batch, with the refinement ladders both of them climb included, we find $972$ against $34\,782$ at a requested $10^{-6}$; at $10^{-8}$, $2\,606$ against $278\,494$ (we count order 4 here, the higher orders sampling more nodes per slab while needing fewer slabs still). At $10^{-4}$ that gap is repaid once a single evaluation of $\mathbb{A}$ costs more than about $35$~ns; from $10^{-6}$ down \magnus\ is ahead whatever a sample costs. A threshold that low is not an exotic price. Interpolating the BS2005-AGS,OP solar model costs between $26$ and $46$~ns per sample, which straddles it---though being vectorized, the interpolant costs \emph{less} per sample the more of them are asked for at once, which favors the code that asks for more. What settles the matter is a cost that does not amortize over a batch; for such a problem we would expect the crossover to leave \figu{smooth_reach} altogether.

Reading across the flavor panels of the exponential profile, the closed form starts cheaper at three flavors than at two, $11$ against $15~\mu$s at the loosest dial, though two flavors takes the lead back everywhere tighter. The cost per slab runs the other way---a $3 \times 3$ exponential costs more than a $2 \times 2$ one---so what crosses the curves is how many slabs each case needs. Our two-flavor case is a stand-in rather than a reduction of the three-flavor one, with near-maximal mixing on $\Delta m_{31}^2$ alone; its commutator with the matter potential is $3.2$ times larger. Since that commutator is precisely what a constant-density slab discards, two flavors carries $7.6$ times the error at a fixed slab count and needs $2.8$ times more slabs to match. The refinement doubles the count, so at the loosest dial the slab deficit is what shows.

\subsection{The Sun: an observable the others do not offer}
\label{sec:solar_comparison}

Section \ref{sec:solar} showed what \magnus\ returns on a tabulated solar model: a few dozen averaged energies in under a second, matching the analytic adiabatic limit to a part in $10^{5}$. What a solar experiment measures is that average, and neither of the other codes exposes it. Both return the coherent probability at a baseline, from which an average has to be assembled. What follows therefore compares an algorithm built for the question against algorithms run harder at it.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure}[t!]
 \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{solar_average_cost.pdf}
 \caption{\label{fig:solar_average_cost}Cost of one averaged survival probability through the Sun, for eight configurations at $5$~MeV. The profile is the tabulated BS2005-AGS,OP model, log-interpolated, and not the exponential fit that {\tt osc\_prob\_*\_sun} assumes: the fit runs high by a factor $2.4$ inside $0.05\,R_\odot$, so the two are different problems and they cost differently. There is no accuracy axis because nothing converges. The evolution is adiabatic the whole way out and every eigenvalue pair has decohered by the time the neutrino arrives, so the level-crossing matrix is the identity and the expression collapses to one eigendecomposition at production and another at detection---exact to about $10^{-15}$, and bit-identical however the routine is tuned. We price that against the same average reached by integration: a single {\tt DOP853} solve of the coherent evolution, from which the phase average is taken over a tapered window at the far end of the ray, both steps inside the quoted time. Its tolerance, ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-8}$, is the loosest that still reproduces the closed form, which it does to between $5$ and $8 \cdot 10^{-5}$; at $10^{-6}$ it is $1.3 \cdot 10^{-2}$ away, and at $10^{-4}$ it returns $0.004$ where the answer is $0.546$. The sterile configurations are projected rather than run: an eV-scale $\Delta m^2_{41}$ puts some $5 \cdot 10^{7}$ oscillations along the ray, and resolving them takes tens of hours. We obtain each projection by extrapolating a measured segment of the same ray, which reproduces the three-flavor cost to $354$~s against $354$~s measured}
\end{figure}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textit{What the average costs.}---\figu{solar_average_cost} prices that answer at $5$~MeV across the scenarios the package covers. The bill follows the number of states and is nearly blind to what the Hamiltonian holds: non-standard interactions cost nothing beyond the run-to-run spread, Lorentz violation adds of order a tenth, and moving from three flavors to $3+1$ or $3+2$ costs more than either does. The reason is that the closed form's work is two eigendecompositions, whose size is set by the number of states; new physics changes the entries of those matrices and not their dimension. This is the one place in the paper where the claim of Section \ref{sec:synopsis}---that the method does not care what is in the Hamiltonian---is shown rather than asserted.

Almost none of that time is the answer. The eigendecompositions are a fraction of a percent of the call; the search for non-adiabatic crossings, which on this profile finds none, is about four fifths of it. \magnus\ spends its time establishing that it is entitled to the shortcut and almost none taking it. We regard that as the right allocation---the shortcut is exact only where the levels do not cross, and a code that assumed rather than checked would be fast and occasionally wrong---but it is worth saying plainly, since the figure is otherwise a measurement of a validity check.

\textit{The same number without the shortcut.}---Integrating the coherent evolution and averaging afterwards is the alternative, and on this ray it is not close. The oscillation phase over one solar radius at $5$~MeV is of order $10^{4}$~radians, a few thousand cycles, every one of which an integrator must resolve and the average then discards. Carrying that out---one {\tt DOP853} solve, then the phase average taken from it---costs four orders of magnitude more than the closed form, the two agreeing on the probability itself to a part in $10^{4}$. A sterile state ends the comparison rather than widening it. An eV-scale $\Delta m^2_{41}$ is some four hundred times the atmospheric splitting, so the ray holds $\sim\!10^{7}$ cycles where three flavors hold $\sim\!10^{5}$, and the same solve runs for days. The closed form does not see the difference, because it never resolves a cycle at all.

\textit{A general-purpose solver on the same file.}---Driven on that model file---{\tt SunASnu} on it, the production radius set through the two-argument {\tt Track}, both {\tt Set\_rel\_error} and {\tt Set\_abs\_error} moved together, and every other setting left at its default, including the evolution basis and the maximum step---{\tt nuSQuIDS} needs of order ten minutes merely to reach a tolerance at which its output is a probability at all, and then a further factor of $N$ to average the phase away. We report this as the cost of asking a general-purpose solver a question it was not built for, not as a defect; the driver is in the repository so that the configuration can be checked and improved on. Across the three tolerances we tried, spanning two decades, the cost roughly triples while the output becomes a probability: at the loosest, survival probabilities run from below $-1$ to above $3$; one decade tighter they sit within a percent of $[0,1]$; only at the tightest does every value fall inside it.

\textbf{The obvious guard passes on every one of those rows.} Summing the flavor probabilities and checking they come to one holds to machine precision even where a survival probability approaches $3$. That is not a bug in the guard: {\tt nuSQuIDS} evolves the density matrix in an SU(3) basis whose identity component is the trace, so the flavor sum is conserved \emph{by construction} however badly the traceless components are integrated. A structural invariant cannot test the thing it is built into. The check that bites is each probability lying in $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Which code to reach for}
\label{sec:which_code}

\tabl{which} says what to run on eleven problems. We include {\tt NuOscProbExact} rather than only \magnus\ because the two are close relatives. They take the same arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian, compose the same ordered product of slab operators, and share units, conventions, and parameter defaults deliberately. The choice between them therefore turns on the shape of the profile rather than on the physics in the Hamiltonian.

\textbf{Reach for the closed form unless the problem takes it outside its range.} Smooth variation is not enough to justify the expansion: the exponential profile of \figu{smooth_reach} varies across every stretch, and the closed form is the cheaper code there wherever an error near $10^{-8}$ will do. Past that the ordering reverses---the two cross at about $7.5 \cdot 10^{-9}$ at three flavors---and the closed form then floors at $6 \cdot 10^{-11}$, two decades above where \magnus\ at order 4 is still descending. What does justify it is structure the staircase has to chase---a layered Earth, a shock front---or an accuracy past about $10^{-10}$, where composing that many factors floors the closed form and \magnus\ continues; or more than four flavors, where no SU($n$) expression exists; or a Hamiltonian that does not factorize as a scalar potential times a fixed projector. An exact algebraic solution beats a truncated series wherever an exact algebraic solution exists. Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product} shows why the two meet: a piecewise-constant composition \emph{is} the Magnus method at second order, so on a staircase the closed form is doing the same arithmetic in fewer operations. What \magnus\ adds is orders 4, 6, and 8 on the same chain, which pays only when there is curvature within a slab for them to resolve.

Two entries in \tabl{which} resist the sorting the rest obey. A supernova shock appears twice with opposite answers; the parameter that separates them is not the physics but the width of the front measured against the oscillation length (Section \ref{sec:shock_comparison}). A tabulated profile belongs to \magnus\ not because tables are special but because the interpolant between samples is smooth, so a method that integrates across a slab has something to integrate.

Because the two packages share conventions, a calculation can be moved from one to the other and run both ways. It is the check to run on any result that matters: agreement between two methods whose failure modes differ is stronger evidence than either one's internal convergence check, which Section \ref{sec:refinement} is careful to describe as evidence rather than proof.

\subsection{Before comparing any two codes' numbers}
\label{sec:conventions_first}

\textbf{Check that the two codes agree in vacuum first.} If they do not, the disagreement is in the solvers. If they agree in vacuum and disagree in matter, it is in the conventions---a matter-potential factor, an electron fraction, a channel index---and no amount of tolerance will close it. A trade-off curve that does not respond to its own dial is reporting a convention difference rather than a solver, which is what \magnus\ and {\tt NuOscProbExact} produce against each other's references through the Earth. Section \ref{sec:the_floor} measures that case, and says what to give each code instead.

\figu{prem_plane} places \magnus\ on three planes of external codes measured by the companion project, whose numbers are frozen in this repository. None of those codes need be installed to redraw the figure. They were timed by the companion project on an Intel Core i5-1334U; we timed \magnus\ on the same machine model in a separate session, so a cross-code cost carries that caveat, while an accuracy, being untimed, does not. The axes are the companion figure's, unchanged, so that the two may be laid side by side, and \magnus's tolerance sweep is drawn only to ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-8}$ for that reason: measured to $10^{-12}$ it reaches $3.9 \cdot 10^{-14}$ at three flavors and $7.9 \cdot 10^{-12}$ at $3+1$, beneath the lower edge of either Earth panel, as its $2\,048$-slab point at $3+1$ also is. Section \ref{sec:prem_comparison} instead compares it against {\tt NuOscProbExact} directly, with both codes installed and each refined against its own tightest setting. The planes carry a caution that transfers to any comparison of this kind.

The top panel and the lower two ask different questions. At constant density every code can be handed the same Hamiltonian, so the comparison measures how exactly each evaluates the probability from it, and an exact answer exists to measure against---one matrix exponential, carrying no discretization at all. Through the Earth there is no single Hamiltonian to hand over: the density changes continuously along the chord and the Hamiltonian with it, and every code---\magnus\ included---must first build its own approximation of the profile. The lower two panels therefore measure how finely a code resolves that profile rather than the exactness of its probability formula.

Each Earth reference is consequently a \emph{converged} PREM solution rather than an exact one: a Richardson extrapolation of a high-precision slab product, which cancels the leading discretization error rather than shrinking it---a full $50$-digit Romberg ladder at three flavors (Richardson applied repeatedly, each rung canceling the next error term), a single $30$-digit pair at $3+1$, where each rung costs far more. Because that combination is built by cutting the chord the way a slab code cuts it, an error in the cutting would sit in both and cancel from the comparison instead of showing up in it. We therefore check it against a calculation sharing none of that machinery: an adaptive integration of the flavor state through the \emph{continuous} profile, restarted at every density jump so that no step straddles one. Run to the double-precision limit that check follows the reference to $9 \cdot 10^{-12}$ and no further---but the limit is the check's, not the reference's, and reading it as a floor would throw away four orders of real accuracy. What settles the question is a second extrapolation at full precision sharing no slab count with the first: at three flavors two disjoint $50$-digit ladders agree to $1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$. The $3+1$ referee is the coarser construction of the two, a single Richardson pair rather than a ladder, and a disjoint pair confirms it to $1.2 \cdot 10^{-12}$; \magnus's tightest point there sits a factor of six above that, so it is resolved, but not by much. Nothing in \figu{prem_plane} is clipped.

\subsubsection{What limits a cross-code comparison through the Earth}
\label{sec:the_floor}

The conventions matched before any curve was drawn are not optional. Every code is given the same oscillation parameters, expressed in the convention that code expects, and the same PREM implementation, chord, and baseline in eV$^{-1}$. The matter potential is the one convention we could not match, for the reason this section gives.

\magnus's charged-current potential sits $1.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ below {\tt NuOscProbExact}'s at the same density, the two codes rounding the nucleon mass differently. Left unmatched, that difference pins \magnus's error against the other code's reference at $2.38 \cdot 10^{-4}$ however tight the tolerance, so the curve comes out flat---points stacked at one height, above every other code, saying nothing about either.

The obvious repair is to match the two potentials, which is what the frozen dataset does for {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt nuCraft} by scaling the density each is handed. Through \magnus's Earth entry points the only handle is the {\tt electron\_fraction} keyword, and passing $0.5000948$ rather than $0.5$ does produce a falling curve. It stops falling at $1.6 \cdot 10^{-7}$, and we first read that floor as the potential match running out in some general way. It is not general. It has one cause, and the cause is worth stating because it is invisible from outside the code.

On a chord \magnus\ derives the average nucleon mass from the composition---$r = n_n/n_p = (1 - Y_e)/Y_e$, layer by layer---because a medium carrying an iron core's $Y_e$ alongside an isoscalar $r$ is matter that cannot exist. The consequence is that $V_{\rm CC}$ is \emph{not} linear in $Y_e$: raising $Y_e$ by $1.9$ parts in $10^{4}$ moves the nucleon mass too, so the potential picks up a further $1.31 \cdot 10^{-7}$ that was never asked for. That number is the floor. Matching a potential through a composition parameter is therefore only ever good to the order at which composition enters the nucleon mass, and no solver setting on either side reaches beneath it.

The repair is not a better scale factor but a different measurement. We give each code its own high-precision reference, built with that code's own constants, and score it at $Y_e = 1/2$ where its conventions are self-consistent---which is what the benchmark manifest asks for in any case. Measured that way on the same chord, \magnus\ reaches $3.9 \cdot 10^{-14}$, four orders below where the matched curve stalled. That is beneath the lower edge of \figu{prem_plane}'s middle panel, which keeps the companion figure's range, so the curve there is drawn only to ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-8}$. The floor was never \magnus's. What that number is, and is not, follows from how it was obtained: a code scored against a reference in its own conventions is being asked how far its own discretization has converged, not how close it sits to another code's answer. Two codes can both reach $10^{-14}$ on \figu{prem_plane} and still differ from one another at $10^{-4}$, which is exactly what the potential mismatch above produces. The ordinate is therefore a self-convergence depth, comparable down a series and not across them, and \magnus\ reaching further down it than the others means that its dial converges further, not that its probabilities are nearer theirs.

The transferable version: \textbf{a cross-code comparison through the Earth is limited by the agreement of the two Earth models long before it is limited by either solver}, and any such comparison reaching past $10^{-7}$ without saying how its potentials were matched is reporting a convention. Matching them is harder than it looks, because the parameter that carries the potential usually carries something else as well; giving each code its own reference sidesteps the question rather than answering it, which is why we do that instead.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsubsection{Timing conventions}

Every code in \figu{prem_plane}, \magnus\ included, is timed by one rule, and it is the companion project's rather than ours: the timed region is a scan of twenty-five steps in $\delta_{\rm CP}$, each step configuring the one parameter that moves and then asking for the whole energy grid, with everything invariant under the scan hoisted outside. What that reports is the cost inside a fit, with each code's caching working as designed, normalized to microseconds per grid point. Each code is dialed over the range its own interface offers, through whatever batched interface it has.

Two conventions in that rule are not fussiness. Ignoring either made \magnus\ look like one of the slowest codes on the plane for reasons having nothing to do with its arithmetic. The first call on a machine pays a one-off compilation of the {\tt Numba} kernels, about $1.5$~s now that there are eight of them; charged to twelve energies that alone is $124\,000~\mu$s per probability. The compiled code is cached on disk, so a later session pays about $10$~ms rather than the full second and a half, but either figure swamps the measurement, so a warm-up pass is run and discarded. A fixed repetition count is the second: it gave the cheapest settings a timed block well under a millisecond, which returns the clock's spread rather than the code's, so the block autoranges until it is long enough to measure. We record the coefficient of variation across blocks for every point and admit none above $0.10$; the worst of \magnus's is $0.076$.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have provided \magnus\ \cite{Magnus}, which computes neutrino oscillation probabilities by propagating the evolution operator with the Magnus expansion, for an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, four, or five flavors, through a matter profile that may vary continuously along the trajectory. Neither the expansion nor the collocation integrators built on it are ours; what we provide is their implementation for this problem, together with the slab machinery, the adaptive refinement, the exactly unitary exponential, the composition with adiabatic transport, the closed-form decohered limit, and the batching that makes a whole scan one call.

Three properties recommend it, each measured rather than asserted. It is \emph{unitary by construction}: every truncation of the series lies in the Lie algebra, so probabilities are non-negative and their rows sum to one to round-off at any accuracy setting, a defect that no accuracy dial moves. The defect does not grow with the baseline, the order, or the tolerance. It is \emph{indifferent to the phase}: a slab is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many cycles it spans, so what sets the discretization is the profile and not the phase. Measured, the cost grows as $\Phi^{0.21}$ against $\Phi^{0.77}$ for a differential-equation solver asked for the same tolerance on the same problem, and \magnus\ is the more accurate of the two there by nearly three orders of magnitude. It is also \emph{indifferent to the physics}: the Hamiltonian is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix, so five flavors, non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, and a term nobody has written down are the same call.

We are equally clear about where it should not be used. It does not treat open systems: decay, decoherence, and collective effects need a density matrix, which no enlargement of the flavor basis provides. It is not the quickest route to a probability in constant or piecewise-constant matter, where a closed form is cheaper by a factor of a few at matched accuracy on a three-flavor Earth chord, and by some $440\times$ at $3+1$. Its tolerances are a stopping criterion rather than an error bound (the same caveat that every adaptive solver carries; Section \ref{sec:refinement} states it in full).

Two directions remain open. The first is borrowed: magnetic resonance has spent sixty years learning to choose a pulse sequence so that whole orders of this same expansion cancel by construction. The neutrino problem offers the same freedom in a different variable. A matter profile is given rather than controlled, but the composition is ours to design---where the slab edges fall, and how each slab is quadratured---and Section \ref{sec:refinement} shows how much that choice already decides. 

The second is a question about composition itself. What a single evaluation costs in accuracy is understood; what a thousand of them chained along a trajectory do to it is not. Whether that error grows with the number of slabs, wanders, or largely cancels is not something the integrator literature answers, since it bounds the discretization error instead. A code that composes thousands of slabs, tracks its own unitarity and carries an arbitrary-precision reference is the natural place to settle it.

None of that touches the case the code was built for: a profile that varies continuously along the path, a Hamiltonian for which no closed form exists, more flavors than a closed form can express, or an observable---the phase-averaged probability of \equ{averaged_varying}---that is better computed than reconstructed. \magnus\ is available from PyPI with {\tt pip install magnuspy}, and from its repository\ \cite{Magnus}.

\section{The Magnus expansion at any order}
\label{app:terms}
\appresetfloats

Section \ref{sec:expansion} gave the recursion; this appendix gives its structure, which is what makes an implementation to order 10 possible without typing $129$ expressions.

Unrolling \equ{recursion}, every term of $\Omega_n$ is a right-nested chain
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:nested}
 \left[\Omega_{m_1}, \left[\Omega_{m_2}, \ldots
 \left[\Omega_{m_j}, \mathbb{A}\right] \ldots \right]\right] \;,
 \quad
 \sum_{i=1}^{j} m_i = n-1 \;,
\end{equation}
carrying the coefficient $B_j/j!$. The terms of the $j$-th group are therefore indexed by the compositions of $n-1$ into $j$ positive parts, of which there are $\binom{n-2}{j-1}$, and since $B_j$ vanishes for odd $j \geq 3$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:term_count}
 \#\,\Omega_n = \sum_{j\,:\,B_j \neq 0} \binom{n-2}{j-1} \;.
\end{equation}
The surviving coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:bernoulli}
 \frac{B_1}{1!} = -\frac{1}{2} , \;
 \frac{B_2}{2!} = \frac{1}{12} , \;
 \frac{B_4}{4!} = -\frac{1}{720} , \;
 \frac{B_6}{6!} = \frac{1}{30240} ,
\end{equation}
and $B_8/8! = -1/1\,209\,600$. Equation (\ref{equ:term_count}) gives $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65, 129$ terms at orders 1 to 10, the count doubling from order 5 onward, which is why the implemented ceiling is a deliberate choice rather than an accident: the terms remain easy to \emph{generate} far beyond it, but the work per slab grows with their number.

The module {\tt magnus.expansionterms} evaluates all of this in exact rational arithmetic at any order, with coefficients returned as fractions so that they can be compared without a tolerance. It shares no code with the numerical core: the test suite regenerates the hard-coded group factors and compares them against the derived ones, and separately evaluates the generated terms numerically on a sampled $\mathbb{A}(l)$ and compares them order by order against what the core produces internally. Agreement is at machine precision for every order from one to ten, covering the hand-written low orders and the generated high ones in one check.

\textit{Why the collocation orders are even.---}Section \ref{sec:quadrature} states that the schemes of \equ{gl} exist only at even order, retaining $\Omega_1$ through $\Omega_{p-2}$ at order $p$. Both statements follow from the sizes of the $\Omega_k$ in the slab width $h$.

Two facts fix those sizes. The first is a parity. Traversing a slab backwards returns the inverse of traversing it forwards, so $\Omega$ changes sign; it does so term by term, since reversal cannot change how many factors of $\mathbb{A}$ a term carries. Measured from the slab midpoint, traversing backwards is the same as replacing $h$ by $-h$, so each $\Omega_k$ contains only odd powers of $h$. The second is a cost. Above $\Omega_1$, every term is a nested commutator of $\mathbb{A}$ with itself at different positions, which vanishes when $\mathbb{A}$ is constant; each therefore carries at least one derivative of $\mathbb{A}$, at the price of one power of $h$ beyond its $k$ integrations. Counting powers alone would place $\Omega_k$ at $h^{k+1}$.

The two meet at odd $k$, where $h^{k+1}$ is an even power that the parity forbids. Such a term scales as $h^{k+2}$ instead. $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ stand alone, at $h$ and $h^3$: the first is no commutator, the second already odd by the naive count. From $\Omega_3$ upward the terms scale in pairs, $\Omega_{2j-1}$ with $\Omega_{2j}$ at $h^{2j+1}$ for $j \geq 2$.

Two consequences follow. A scheme of order $p$ carries a local error $\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})$, so it must retain every term larger than that: $\Omega_1$ alone at $p = 2$, $\Omega_1$ through $\Omega_{p-2}$ for $p \geq 4$. The error a scheme makes on one slab is then the size of the largest term it leaves out, which is an odd power of $h$. The trajectory holds $N$ such slabs, whose errors accumulate, so an error $h^q$ on each leaves $h^{q-1}$ over the whole path. Odd $q$ gives even $q-1$, which is why no odd order is available.

We confirm the scalings numerically. Fitting $\lVert \Omega_k \rVert$ against $h$ on single slabs of the exponential profile of \figu{phase_vs_profile}, over widths from $47$ to $750$~km of its $3\,000$-km baseline, returns exponents $1.00$, $3.05$, $5.04$, $5.03$, $7.03$ and $7.03$ for $\Omega_1$ through $\Omega_6$. A randomly drawn $3 \times 3$ Hamiltonian with no physics in it returns the same six, each within $0.01$ of the integer. Each scheme also differs from the truncation it stands for by the next pair of terms: at a slab width of $L/16$, $\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(6)}$ minus $\Omega_1 + \Omega_2 + \Omega_3$ agrees with $\Omega_4$ to four digits; $\Omega_{\rm GL}^{(8)}$ minus $\Omega_1 + \cdots + \Omega_4$ agrees with $\Omega_5 + \Omega_6$.

Higher order buys a genuinely faster convergence rate in the slab width---measured against a tight reference on a smooth Hamiltonian with a single slab, so that these are \emph{local} rates on the quadrature path rather than the global ones of Section \ref{sec:quadrature}, orders 4, 6 and 8 converge as $h^{6.6}$, $h^{8.3}$ and $h^{10.2}$---but the cost per slab grows faster than the order does; narrowing the slabs at order 4 or 6 usually reaches a given accuracy for less total work. \magnus\ warns when an order above six is requested. More fundamentally, absolute convergence is guaranteed only while $\int\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert\,dl < \pi$, and past that bound raising the order is no longer the reliable move. The answer is narrower slabs.

\section{The test suite}
\label{app:tests}
\appresetfloats

The repository carries $1250$ tests covering $93\%$ of the library, run on Python $3.10$ through $3.13$ on every push and every pull request, together with a clean-room installation of the built wheel. They are not packaged with the library; from a clone,
\begin{verbatim}
pip install -e ".[test]"
pytest
\end{verbatim}

The suite is not only a regression net. One part of it checks the mathematics against something external to the implementation---the Bernoulli recursion coded independently, closed-form vacuum and constant-density expressions, a tight {\tt DOP853} integration, an exact {\tt expm} where the profile makes it exact. Another checks the properties a probability must have whatever the Hamiltonian: unitarity, the behavior of reversed channels, agreement between the scalar, vectorized and compiled paths. A third asserts \emph{identities} rather than tolerances---the batched scan against the per-point path, a parallel run against a serial one---so that an optimization which changed an answer fails rather than passes quietly. A fourth asserts on the \emph{distribution} of the error over random profiles rather than on individual cases. A fifth guards the layout itself: the documented file tree is generated from a table and checked against the repository, so adding a file fails the suite until the table follows it; the same mechanism keeps this paper's directory in the tree.
