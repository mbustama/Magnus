\title{\magnus: neutrino oscillation probabilities for any Hamiltonian,\\
any number of flavors, any density profile}

\author{Mauricio Bustamante\corref{author}}
\ead{mbustamante@nbi.ku.dk}
\cortext[author]{ORCID: \href{http://orcid.org/0000-0001-6923-0865}{0000-0001-6923-0865}.}
\address{Niels Bohr International Academy, Niels Bohr Institute,\\University of Copenhagen, DK-2100 Copenhagen, Denmark}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}

A neutrino created with one flavor can \textit{oscillate} and be detected later with another. Where the matter it crosses has constant density, the probability of this happening is a closed form. Where the density varies continuously---inside the Sun, through a supernova envelope, along a chord through the Earth---it is not, and the usual recourse is to replace the profile by a staircase of constant steps, or to integrate the evolution equation numerically. We take a third route. \magnus\ propagates the evolution operator with the \textit{Magnus expansion}: over each segment of the trajectory the operator is written as a single exponential whose argument is a truncated series of time-ordered integrals of the Hamiltonian, evaluated by Gauss--Legendre quadrature. Every truncation of that series remains in the Lie algebra, so the operator is unitary \emph{by construction} rather than to the accuracy of the truncation, at any order and any setting. The decisive practical consequence is that a segment is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many oscillation cycles it spans: what sets its width is how fast the \emph{profile} changes, not how fast the \emph{phase} winds. A differential-equation solver has the opposite constraint, and pays for every radian. Nothing in the method fixes the size of the Hamiltonian or what it contains, so vacuum, matter, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation, sterile states, five flavors, combinations of these, and a Hamiltonian nobody has written down yet are one and the same call. \magnus\ also returns the phase-averaged, decohered probability---the quantity a solar experiment measures---in closed form, without propagating the $10^{4}$ radians that separate production from detection. We set out the method, the code, and a measured comparison against two other public codes, and we say plainly where each of them wins.

\end{abstract}

{\em Nature of problem:} \\
Computation of neutrino flavor oscillation probabilities for an arbitrary number of flavors, in media whose density varies continuously along the trajectory, and for Hamiltonians that are time- or position-dependent --- including scenarios for which no closed form exists. \\ \\

{\em Solution method:}\\
The evolution operator is propagated with the Magnus expansion. Truncated time-ordered integrals of the Hamiltonian are evaluated by Gauss--Legendre quadrature over a chain of position slabs and exponentiated; every truncation remains in the Lie algebra, so the operator is unitary by construction. Slab widths are chosen adaptively from a requested tolerance, and slab edges are placed on the discontinuities of a layered profile. Where the accumulated phase is extreme, an adiabatic transport is composed with exact local Magnus patches across the non-adiabatic windows. The phase-averaged, decohered limit is available in closed form, without propagating. \\ \\

%%%%

The Hamiltonian must be Hermitian and the evolution unitary: decay to invisible states, absorption, decoherence as an open-system process, and collective (self-interaction) effects lie outside the method and need a density-matrix or nonlinear solver instead. \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Neutrinos are created and detected in weak interactions as flavor states ($\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, $\nu_\tau$) but they propagate as superpositions of propagation states\ \cite{Pontecorvo:1967fh, Maki:1962mu, Bilenky:2004sn, Fantini:2018itu}. Because the superposition evolves, a neutrino created with one flavor has a non-zero probability of being detected with another. The observation of that in solar, atmospheric, reactor, and accelerator neutrinos led to the discovery of neutrino mass and of flavor mixing in leptons\ \cite{Kajita:2016cak, McDonald:2016ixn}.

Computing the probability means solving
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:schrodinger}
 i\,\frac{d}{dl}\,\nu(l) = \mathbb{H}(l)\,\nu(l) \;,
\end{equation}
with $\mathbb{H}$ the Hamiltonian in flavor space and $l$ the distance along the trajectory, which for relativistic neutrinos stands in for the elapsed time. Where $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on $l$, the solution is $\nu(l) = e^{-i \mathbb{H} l}\,\nu(0)$ and the problem is closed. Oscillations in vacuum and in matter of constant density have exact solutions. The companion code {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} evaluates them for an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, and four flavors (as far as the SU($n$) constructions reach).

One quantity governs everything that follows. The \emph{accumulated phase} along a trajectory is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:phase}
 \Phi
 \equiv
 \int_{l_0}^{l_1} \left[\lambda_{\rm max}(l) - \lambda_{\rm min}(l)\right] dl \;,
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\rm max}$ and $\lambda_{\rm min}$ the largest and smallest instantaneous eigenvalues of $\mathbb{H}(l)$. It is the total phase difference the fastest-separating pair of propagation states builds up between production and detection, measured in radians. It is what any method that follows the oscillation must resolve. In vacuum at three flavors it reduces to the familiar $\Delta m^2_{31} L / 2E$. For a $1$~GeV neutrino crossing the Earth it is of order $10$; for a $5$~MeV neutrino leaving the Sun, some $1.3 \cdot 10^{4}$; and for a TeV neutrino from $100$~Mpc, some $10^{15}$ radians.

The difficulty this paper addresses begins where the density varies \emph{continuously} along the path. Then $\mathbb{H}(l)$ does not commute with itself at different positions, the exponential of the integral is not the solution, and two standard recourses remain. One is to replace the profile by a staircase: cut the path into slabs of constant density, solve each in closed form, and compose the operators in order. The other is to integrate \equ{schrodinger} numerically with a differential-equation solver. Each has a characteristic failure. The staircase converges only as fast as a piecewise-constant approximation to a smooth function (second order in the slab width). An adaptive solver must instead resolve the oscillation phase step by step, so its cost grows with $\Phi$. Neither difficulty is about the physics in the Hamiltonian; both are about the shape of the profile and the size of $\Phi$.

We introduce \magnus\ \cite{Magnus}, a code that computes oscillation probabilities by propagating the evolution operator with the \textit{Magnus expansion}\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz, Blanes:2008xlr}. Over a segment of the trajectory the operator is written as a \emph{single} exponential, $\mathbb{U} = \exp(\Omega)$, whose argument is a series of time-ordered integrals of nested commutators of the Hamiltonian. Two properties follow. Between them they are the reason for the code.

First, \textbf{truncating the series is exact for the group even where it is approximate for the answer}. Each term of $\Omega$ is a real combination of nested commutators of anti-Hermitian matrices and is therefore itself anti-Hermitian, so any truncation stays in the Lie algebra and its exponential is \emph{exactly} unitary. Probabilities are non-negative and their rows sum to one at machine precision, at any truncation order, any quadrature accuracy, and any slab count. Figure~\ref{fig:validation} shows this beside the accuracy. The two live at different scales, the lower one not moving when the upper one does.

Second---and this is the property the whole code is built around---\textbf{a segment is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many oscillation cycles it spans}. When $\mathbb{H}$ does not vary, every nested commutator vanishes, the series terminates at its first term, and $\exp(-i\mathbb{H}\Delta l)$ is not an approximation to be refined but the answer. What forces a segment to be narrow is therefore the variation of the \emph{profile} across it, not the phase accumulated inside it. A differential-equation solver has the opposite constraint. Section~\ref{sec:phase_vs_profile} measures both statements.

Three further properties follow from the same construction and are not shared by the closed forms. There is no ceiling on the number of flavors. The SU($n$) constructions that give closed-form probabilities need the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian in radicals, which the Abel--Ruffini theorem forbids past four. Nothing in the Magnus expansion refers to the size of the matrix at all; we ship two, three, four, and five flavors. There is no ceiling on what the Hamiltonian contains. It is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix, so non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, sterile states (or all of them at once), or a term nobody has written down are the same call with a different matrix. We return the \emph{decohered} limit (the phase-averaged probability a solar or astrophysical measurement actually returns) in closed form, without propagating the phase that is about to be averaged away.

Where a closed form exists and the accumulated phase is large, the closed form wins. We measure it in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}: on a piecewise-constant PREM chord {\tt NuOscProbExact} is about $20$ times cheaper than \magnus\ at three flavors, and about $400$ times at $3+1$. What \magnus\ buys is what lies outside it: accuracy past the point where a piecewise-constant discretization stalls, an arbitrary varying profile, a Hamiltonian nobody has diagonalized, five flavors, and observables we return rather than reconstruct from a scan of instantaneous probabilities.

Section \ref{sec:method} presents the method (where this paper and its companion genuinely diverge). Section \ref{sec:code} describes the code, Section \ref{sec:examples} shows it in use, Section \ref{sec:performance} measures its speed and precision, and Section \ref{sec:comparison} compares it against two other public codes on problems refereed by a third method. Section \ref{sec:conclusions} concludes; the appendices hold supporting detail: the structure of the expansion, the test suite, the notebooks, and the conventions behind the Earth comparison.

\subsection{Overview of the method}
\label{sec:overview}

The Magnus expansion is due to Magnus\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz} and has a large literature in numerical analysis, where it is one of the standard geometric integrators---methods that preserve a structure of the problem exactly rather than to within the truncation error\ \cite{Iserles:2000acta, Hairer:2006gni}. \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr} reviews it, and \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit} derives the commutator-free Gauss--Legendre integrators that \magnus\ uses by default. None of that is ours. What we provide is its implementation for neutrino flavor evolution: the expansion carried to order ten, the quadratures, the slab chain and its adaptive refinement, an exactly unitary matrix exponential, the composition with adiabatic transport for extreme accumulated phase, the closed-form decohered limit, and the engineering that makes a whole scan over energy or arrival direction one batched call rather than thousands of separate ones.

The expansion has been used for neutrino oscillations before. We do not claim the idea. What has not been available is a general-purpose, tested, documented implementation. It takes an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at an arbitrary number of flavors, chooses its own discretization from a requested tolerance, and places its slab edges on the discontinuities of a layered profile. It also says which of its six internal engines answered a call---the independent methods that can serve a request, listed in Section \ref{sec:engines}---and reports, rather than assuming, when the evidence for its own convergence is weak.

Two limits bound everything that follows. The Hamiltonian must be Hermitian, which is what makes the evolution unitary and the system closed; and the Magnus series converges absolutely only while $\int \lVert \mathbb{H} \rVert_2\, dl < \pi$ over the interval the expansion is applied to\ \cite{Moan:2008conv}. The first is a restriction on the physics and Section \ref{sec:limitations} says what it excludes. The second is not, because the interval is the code's to choose: a long trajectory is cut into as many slabs as the criterion needs, and \magnus\ checks the criterion on each and warns when a slab is outside it.

\subsection{Relation to other oscillation codes}
\label{sec:relation}

Several mature codes compute oscillation probabilities. They overlap less than their common subject suggests. {\tt GLoBES}\ \cite{GLoBES, Huber:2007ji} is an experiment-simulation framework in which probabilities are one layer inside a package built for fluxes, cross-sections and detector response. {\tt Prob3++}\ \cite{Prob3pp} evaluates the analytic three-flavor probability in matter\ \cite{Barger:1980tf} and is long established in atmospheric analyses. {\tt nuCraft}\ \cite{nuCraft} integrates numerically along a chord through the Earth. {\tt NuFast-LBL}\ \cite{Denton:2024pzc} and {\tt NuFast-Earth}\ \cite{Denton:2025oxf} are fast specializations of the three-flavor case. {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} evaluates closed-form SU($n$) expansions of the evolution operator for an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, and four flavors, and composes them across slabs of constant density. {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, Arguelles:2021twb, NuSQuIDS} is the most general: it propagates a density matrix rather than a state, which admits interactions, attenuation and open-system terms that no closed form reaches, at the cost of solving a differential equation.

\magnus\ sits between the last two. The difference from each is structural rather than a matter of degree. Against a closed-form slab code, the difference is what happens \emph{inside} a slab: {\tt NuOscProbExact} freezes the Hamiltonian there and is exact for the frozen one, while \magnus\ integrates the Hamiltonian across the slab and is exact to the order of its truncation for the real one. Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product} shows that the two coincide exactly at second order---a piecewise-constant composition with the density read at each slab's midpoint \emph{is} the order-two Magnus method---so what we add on the same slab chain is the higher orders, and the flavor counts and Hamiltonians that a closed form cannot express. Against {\tt nuSQuIDS}, the difference is that a unitary state-vector method cannot represent an open system at all, and pays nothing for the generality it does not have.

Section \ref{sec:comparison} measures \magnus\ against {\tt NuOscProbExact} and {\tt nuSQuIDS} directly, with every case refereed by a method that is neither code's, and states where each of the three wins.

\section{Neutrino oscillation recap}
\label{sec:recap}

Let $\nu$ be the flavor state of a neutrino, an $n$-component column vector, and $\mathbb{H}$ the $n \times n$ Hamiltonian in flavor space. We use natural units, $c = \hbar = 1$, with energies in eV and distances in eV$^{-1}$, so that $\mathbb{H}L$ is dimensionless. Because neutrinos are relativistic we identify the elapsed time with the distance traveled and write both as $l$. The state obeys \equ{schrodinger}; its solution defines the evolution operator $\mathbb{U}(l_1, l_0)$ through $\nu(l_1) = \mathbb{U}(l_1,l_0)\, \nu(l_0)$, with $\mathbb{U}(l_0,l_0) = \mathbb{1}$. The probability that a neutrino born as $\nu_\alpha$ is detected as $\nu_\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:probability}
 P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}(l_1, l_0)
 =
 \left\lvert \left[\mathbb{U}(l_1,l_0)\right]_{\beta\alpha} \right\rvert^2 \;.
\end{equation}
We take $\mathbb{H}$ Hermitian throughout; $\mathbb{U}$ is then unitary and $\sum_\beta P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} = 1$ for every $\alpha$. Flavor indices run over $e, \mu, \tau$ at $n=3$ and over $e, \mu, \tau, s_1, s_2$ at $n = 4$ and $5$, the last two being sterile.

In vacuum the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h_vacuum}
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm vac}(E)
 =
 \frac{1}{2E}\, \mathbb{R}\, \mathbb{M}^2\, \mathbb{R}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation}
with $\mathbb{M}^2 = {\rm diag}(0, \Delta m^2_{21}, \Delta m^2_{31}, \ldots)$ and $\mathbb{R}$ the mixing matrix, parametrized by the usual angles and CP-violating phases. In matter it acquires the Wolfenstein term\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1977ue, Mikheev:1986gs}
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h_matter}
 \mathbb{H}(E, l)
 =
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm vac}(E)
 +
 V_{\rm CC}(l)\, \mathbb{P} \;,
 \quad
 V_{\rm CC} = \sqrt{2}\, G_F\, n_e(l) \;,
\end{equation}
where $n_e$ is the electron number density and $\mathbb{P}$ is a fixed matrix.

The projector $\mathbb{P}$ is where the flavor count enters. At three flavors $\mathbb{P} = {\rm diag}(1,0,0)$: the neutral-current potential is common to the three active flavors and cancels from the probabilities. With a sterile state it no longer cancels, because a sterile neutrino feels neither potential, and $\mathbb{P} = {\rm diag}(1, 0, 0, r/2, \ldots)$ with $r$ the ratio of neutrons to protons. \magnus\ builds $\mathbb{P}$ in exactly one place, {\tt matter.matter\_potential\_projector}, for every flavor count; writing it out a second time by hand is what once produced a wrong answer in the non-standard-interaction route. For antineutrinos the mixing matrix is conjugated \emph{and} the potential changes sign; the second is applied once, inside the potential constructor, and doing only one of the two returns a plausible wrong answer\ \cite{MagnusDocs}.

Everything that follows treats $\mathbb{H}(E, l)$ as given. It is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix. The method never asks where it came from.

\section{The method}

\subsection{The Magnus expansion}
\label{sec:expansion}

Write $\mathbb{A}(l) \equiv -i\,\mathbb{H}(l)$, so that \equ{schrodinger} reads $d\mathbb{U}/dl = \mathbb{A}(l)\,\mathbb{U}$. Where $\mathbb{A}$ commutes with itself at different positions the solution is $\exp\!\int \mathbb{A}\,dl$; where it does not, that expression is wrong. The time-ordered exponential that replaces it is not a single exponential at all. The Magnus expansion restores one\ \cite{Magnus:1954zz}: it writes
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:magnus}
 \mathbb{U}(l, l_0) = \exp\left[\Omega(l, l_0)\right] \;,
 \qquad
 \Omega = \sum_{k \geq 1} \Omega_k \;,
\end{equation}
\emph{exactly}, with the terms generated by a recursion in the Bernoulli numbers $B_j$ (in the $B_1 = -1/2$ convention)\ \cite{Blanes:2008xlr},
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:recursion}
 \Omega_1 = \int_{l_0}^{l} \mathbb{A}\,ds \;,
 \quad
 \Omega_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{B_j}{j!} \int_{l_0}^{l} S_n^{(j)}\, ds \;,
\end{equation}
where $S_n^{(1)} = [\Omega_{n-1}, \mathbb{A}]$ and $S_n^{(j)} = \sum_{m=1}^{n-j} [\Omega_m, S_{n-m}^{(j-1)}]$.

We write the first three terms out, since they are the ones the reader will recognize and the ones the implementation spells:
\begin{align}
 \label{equ:omega123}
 \Omega_1 &= \int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds\, \mathbb{A}(s) \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega_2 &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds_1 \int_{l_0}^{s_1}\! ds_2\,
 \left[\mathbb{A}(s_1), \mathbb{A}(s_2)\right] \;, \\
 \Omega_3 &= \frac{1}{6}\int_{l_0}^{l}\! ds_1 \int_{l_0}^{s_1}\! ds_2
 \int_{l_0}^{s_2}\! ds_3 \Big(
 \left[\mathbb{A}_1, [\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_3]\right] \nonumber \\
 &\hspace{3.1cm} + \left[\mathbb{A}_3, [\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_1]\right]
 \Big) \;, \nonumber
\end{align}
with $\mathbb{A}_i \equiv \mathbb{A}(s_i)$. \ref{app:terms} gives the structure at any order. Two facts about the recursion matter in practice. The odd Bernoulli numbers vanish for $j \geq 3$, so whole commutator groups drop out and only $j = 1, 2, 4, 6, \ldots$ contribute. Every term unrolls to a right-nested chain $[\Omega_{m_1}, [\Omega_{m_2}, \ldots [\Omega_{m_j}, \mathbb{A}]\ldots]]$ with $\sum_i m_i = n-1$, so the terms of the $j$-th group are indexed by the compositions of $n-1$ into $j$ positive parts and there are $\binom{n-2}{j-1}$ of them. Their number roughly doubles per order: $\Omega_7$ has $17$ terms and $\Omega_{10}$ has $129$.

The first term already carries a case with a literature of its own. Where $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on position, $\Omega_1 = -i\mathbb{H}\Delta l$ is the whole of the expansion, since every higher term is a nested commutator of $\mathbb{H}$ with itself and vanishes identically. Its exponential is then the exact evolution operator, not a truncation of one. Evaluating it is exactly the problem {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact, Bustamante:2019ggq} solves in closed form: expanding $\mathbb{H}$ and $e^{-i\mathbb{H}L}$ in the generators of SU($n$) returns the probability from traces alone, without ever constructing an eigenvector, and exactly to round-off at every flavor count for which the eigenvalues can be written down. Composing one such operator per slab, in the order the neutrino meets them, is the standard slab treatment of a piecewise-constant profile. We take that correspondence further in Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}, where it turns out to be an identity rather than an analogy: reading the density at each slab's midpoint makes the slab composition the order-two Magnus method, term for term.

We implement \equ{recursion} through order ten. Orders one to six are written out inline; beyond that the terms are generated from the recursion. A separate module, {\tt expansionterms}, derives the same terms symbolically in exact rational arithmetic at \emph{any} order and shares no code with the numerical core, so the two agreeing is evidence rather than a tautology; the test suite regenerates the hard-coded coefficients and compares (\ref{app:tests}).

\subsection{Truncation is exactly unitary}
\label{sec:unitarity}

$\mathbb{A} = -i\mathbb{H}$ is anti-Hermitian for Hermitian $\mathbb{H}$. The commutator of two anti-Hermitian matrices is again anti-Hermitian. Every $\Omega_k$ in \equ{recursion} is a real-coefficient combination of nested commutators of $\mathbb{A}$ with itself, so every $\Omega_k$ is anti-Hermitian, as is any partial sum. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:unitary}
 \Omega^\dagger = -\Omega
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \left[\exp(\Omega)\right]^\dagger \exp(\Omega) = \mathbb{1}
\end{equation}
for \emph{every} truncation order and every quadrature accuracy. Truncating early costs accuracy; it never costs norm. This is the sense in which the method is a geometric integrator: the exact solution lies in the unitary group. The approximation is constructed to lie there too rather than to approach it.

The guarantee is only as good as the exponential that realizes it. An anti-Hermitian $\Omega$ exponentiated by a general-purpose Pad\'e routine comes back unitary to the accuracy of that routine, not exactly. \magnus\ therefore computes $\exp(\Omega)$ from the eigendecomposition of the Hermitian matrix $\mathbb{K} = i\Omega$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:expm}
 \exp(\Omega) = \mathbb{V}\, {\rm diag}\!\left(e^{-i\lambda_k}\right) \mathbb{V}^\dagger \;,
 \qquad
 \mathbb{K} = \mathbb{V}\, {\rm diag}(\lambda_k)\, \mathbb{V}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation}
which is a diagonal phase conjugated by a unitary matrix. Section \ref{sec:exponential} describes the compiled kernel that evaluates \equ{expm} at two and three flavors.

We state the residual rather than round it away: neither route is unitary to the last bit, because $\mathbb{V}$ itself is computed in floating point. Measured, $\lVert \mathbb{U}^\dagger \mathbb{U} - \mathbb{1}\rVert$ is $4 \cdot 10^{-16}$ for a single $3 \times 3$ and $4 \cdot 10^{-15}$ for a stack of $4096$, and on \magnus's own probability output it degrades only from $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ to $1.6 \cdot 10^{-11}$ across four decades in the number of points, at two through five flavors. What the construction removes is the \emph{growth} of a unitarity defect with the accuracy setting, the baseline, or the truncation order, which is what an integrator that only approaches the unitary manifold cannot avoid.

\subsection{Slab width follows the profile, not the phase}
\label{sec:phase_vs_profile}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure*}[t!]
 \centering

\end{figure*}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure~\ref{fig:phase_vs_profile} measures, three ways, the property that decides which problems belong to this method. We take the first two here, each answering a different objection; the third, what the truncation order buys at fixed phase, belongs with the composition, so we take it in Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}.

\smallskip

\textit{A constant Hamiltonian costs one slab.}---If $\mathbb{H}$ does not depend on $l$ then every commutator in \equ{recursion} vanishes identically, $\Omega = -i\mathbb{H}\Delta l$ exactly, so the expansion terminates at its first term. The evolution operator over the whole interval is one exponential, whatever the accumulated phase. The upper right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile} tests this over six decades of phase, from $1.7$ to $3.8 \cdot 10^{6}$ radians: one slab reproduces an exact exponentiation of the same Hamiltonian to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-16}$ at the bottom of that span and $6.2 \cdot 10^{-10}$ at the top. What grows is $\Phi\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ the double-precision unit round-off, which reaches $8.5 \cdot 10^{-10}$ over the same span. That is the accuracy with which the \emph{angle} is representable and has nothing to do with the expansion; a closed form evaluated in double precision at the same phase carries the same limit. We carry the reference at fifty decimal digits for that reason: a double-precision exponentiation is itself in error by $2 \cdot 10^{-10}$ there, comparable to the residual under measurement.

\smallskip

\textit{A varying Hamiltonian costs slabs according to how fast it varies.}---The corrections $\Omega_2, \Omega_3, \ldots$ are built from commutators of $\mathbb{A}$ at different positions, and those vanish to the extent that $\mathbb{A}$ is the same at both. A slab must therefore be narrow enough that the \emph{Hamiltonian} is nearly constant across it. It need not be narrow enough to resolve the oscillation. A stepping solver has no such distinction: it must place steps inside every oscillation whether or not the profile is doing anything. Its cost therefore grows with the phase. 

The lower left panel puts numbers on the difference. We hold the profile fixed and sweep the energy over $41$ points from $20$ to $0.02$~GeV, which carries the accumulated phase from $6$ to $952$ radians. Every configuration runs at one tolerance of $10^{-8}$---the solver included---chosen before the sweep and held across all of it, with the slab count and the samples per slab left to the package to refine from it. That is how the code is used: a tolerance is set once and the energies are swept under it. We retune nothing per energy, and we fix neither the slab count nor the samples per slab. The curves step rather than glide as a result. The code meets a tolerance by raising the slab count in discrete stages until two successive stages agree, and every appreciable jump in cost coincides with one of those stages. The staircase is a property of the method, not of the way we drove it.

A tolerance is a request and not a guarantee, and the seven answer it differently. Asked for $10^{-8}$, {\tt DOP853} comes back with $2.4 \cdot 10^{-7}$ at worst, missing what it asked for by more than an order of magnitude, while every Magnus configuration comes in under it, order ten by nearly three orders of magnitude. What the panel compares is therefore cost at a common \emph{request}, and \magnus\ is the cheaper answer at the more accurate one. The reference resolves those deviations with room to spare: each configuration's worst is more than ten times the reference's own residual uncertainty at that energy. At the quiet end of the sweep the highest orders fall below that uncertainty, and of those we can say only that they are at least as accurate as it is. One choice in how we run the solver is worth stating, since it favors it: we ask for the whole evolution matrix in a single call rather than one column at a time, which is worth a factor of about three.

On that footing the difference between the two is one of kind and not of degree. {\tt DOP853} grows steeply with the phase, as $\Phi^{0.77}$: a hundred-and-fiftyfold in phase costs it fiftyfold in time, from about $5$ to $270$~ms. The Gauss--Legendre configurations are nearly indifferent to it. Orders four and six both grow as roughly $\Phi^{0.2}$, from under $3$~ms to about $12$~ms, so the gap to the solver widens from a factor of $2$ at the low-phase end to about $20$ at the high one. It widens while they are also the more accurate answer. A Magnus cost is set by the profile and a solver's by the phase, and the harder the problem becomes in phase, the further apart the two move.

Order two costs more than either, and not because the phase drives it harder. Its slab count grows the most slowly of the three, as $\Phi^{0.32}$, and below $0.2$~GeV it stops growing altogether. The Hamiltonian splits into a vacuum term, which sets the phase and is the same at every point of the trajectory, and a matter term, which varies along it and does not depend on the energy. A slab has to resolve the variation, and the variation is entirely in the second of the two. Lowering the energy raises the phase and leaves the profile untouched, which is what separates the two. Between $0.1$ and $0.02$~GeV the phase grows fivefold, and twenty thousand slabs reach $10^{-8}$ at both ends. What makes second order expensive is that it converges as $N^{-2}$ and needs thousands of slabs at any phase. Its worst point falls in the middle of the sweep, where the MSW resonance for $\Delta m^2_{31}$ sits on the trajectory. Below $0.27$~GeV that resonance would require a density above the central $100$~g~cm$^{-3}$; it leaves the profile, and the slab count falls with it. An antineutrino sees the opposite sign of the matter potential and so meets no resonance at all; run against that control, the resonance is worth about $1.7$ in slabs at second order, and orders four and six meet it more weakly.

Both families flatten at the low-phase end, and that flattening is what a cost set by the profile looks like. Up to about $40$ radians the order-six slab count does not move: it holds near a hundred and fifty slabs, never coarser than a tenth of the profile's scale height, while the phase grows sevenfold underneath it. That floor is set by the profile and not by the refinement itself. On a constant profile at the same energy the same call settles on two slabs, and flattening the exponential tenfold in scale height more than halves the count. {\tt DOP853} flattens too, but its floor moves the other way: steepening the profile at fixed energy \emph{lowers} its step count, because what it must resolve is the phase, and a steeper profile leaves less matter along the path to accumulate any. The two discretizations answer to different things, which is the whole of the argument.

The cumulative-quadrature configurations run the other way. Left to choose the samples per slab for itself, Simpson settles at sixty-odd of the Hamiltonian per slab over most of the sweep and reaches its ceiling of $500$ only at the highest phases, where Gauss--Legendre collocation holds at two throughout. At the far end order ten therefore spends some $4 \cdot 10^{5}$ evaluations of $\mathbb{H}$ where order six on the default path spends $3 \cdot 10^{3}$, and the cost follows: over a minute per probability against about $12$~ms. Their slab counts grow with the phase at much the same rate as the Gauss--Legendre ones, near $\Phi^{0.45}$; what separates them is the samples per slab, which Gauss--Legendre holds fixed and Simpson grows as $\Phi^{0.4}$. It is the quadrature and not the truncation that sets their discretization, and so their cost tracks the phase as steeply as a stepping solver's, and in fact more so---the very dependence the method exists to avoid. These are not configurations to reach for. The default integrator is the commutator-free Gauss--Legendre one; it stops at order six because no such scheme exists above it, and it is what wins here. The curves mark where the method stops paying. Converging in fewer slabs and costing less time are different things, and only the second is what a caller pays.

\smallskip

\textit{The consequence.}---For the Sun at $5$~MeV, where $\Phi \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{4}$ radians, the difference is not a constant factor but the difference between a calculation and a research project. Section \ref{sec:hybrid} describes the strategy that handles that regime. Section \ref{sec:solar} describes the observable that removes the need for it.

\subsection{Quadrature: commutator-free Gauss--Legendre integrators}
\label{sec:quadrature}

Evaluating \equ{recursion} means sampling $\mathbb{A}$ inside each slab, for which we offer two families of rule. The default follows \Ref\ \cite{Blanes:2000bit}: orders two, four and six are reached from only one, two, or three evaluations of $\mathbb{A}$ per slab, taken at the Gauss--Legendre nodes, with the quadrature order matched exactly to the truncation order and no separate commutator bookkeeping. With $h$ the slab width and $\mathbb{A}_i$ the Hamiltonian at node $i$,
\begin{align}
 \label{equ:gl}
 \Omega^{(2)} &= h\, \mathbb{A}_1 \;, \nonumber \\
 \Omega^{(4)} &= \frac{h}{2}\left(\mathbb{A}_1 + \mathbb{A}_2\right)
 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{12}\, h^2 \left[\mathbb{A}_2, \mathbb{A}_1\right] \;,
\end{align}
and similarly at order six on three nodes. Because the quadrature is matched to the truncation, this needs far fewer Hamiltonian evaluations than a general rule for the same accuracy. It is simultaneously the fastest and the most accurate choice whenever $\mathbb{A}$ is smooth inside a slab. Slab edges aligned to a layered profile (Section \ref{sec:refinement}) make that the common case even across the Earth, where PREM is smooth between shell boundaries and the edges can be put on the boundaries themselves.

The alternative is cumulative quadrature: sample $\mathbb{A}$ on a uniform grid inside each slab and integrate with the trapezoid or Simpson rule. It is slower for the same accuracy on a smooth profile but fully general. It is therefore the safer choice if $\mathbb{A}$ has a kink or a jump \emph{inside} a slab, where Gauss--Legendre loses its order advantage. It is also the only route above order six, since the commutator-free schemes exist only to that order; requesting more from them raises an error rather than quietly returning an order-six result, which would make a requested order-eight run silently wrong.

We default to order four. The adaptive refinement already converts a higher order into fewer slabs on its own, so order and tolerance interact: raising the order pays only once the tolerance is tight enough to make the extra work per slab worthwhile. Measured across Earth, solar, and exponential-profile cases, order six runs $0.89$ to $1.04$ times the wall time of order four at a requested $10^{-4}$, and $1.08$ to $1.93$ times \emph{faster} at $10^{-8}$. Dropping to order two is almost never worthwhile: at $10^{-8}$ on an Earth chord it needs thousands of slabs where order six needs about a hundred.

\subsection{Composition, and what a closed-form slab code is doing}
\label{sec:order_is_the_slab_product}

We cut a trajectory into $N$ slabs, evaluate the expansion independently in each, and compose the operators in position order,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:composition}
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm tot}
 =
 \mathbb{U}_N \cdots \mathbb{U}_2\, \mathbb{U}_1 \;,
\end{equation}
with the slab crossed \emph{first} standing \emph{rightmost}, since the operators act on the state to their right. Because each factor is unitary by construction, so is the product, however many factors there are and whatever the accuracy of each. The ordering matters physically whenever the slab Hamiltonians do not commute---an asymmetric profile together with a non-zero CP phase---and we check it in the test suite against a two-slab case done in matrix arithmetic alone, with no quadrature involved.

Equation (\ref{equ:composition}) is also where this method and a closed-form slab code meet. They meet more closely than either literature suggests. Take \equ{gl} at order two: $\Omega^{(2)} = h\,\mathbb{A}_1$, and the single Gauss--Legendre node of a one-point rule is the slab \emph{midpoint}, so $\mathbb{U}_j = \exp[-i\,\mathbb{H}(l_j^{\rm mid})\,h]$. That is precisely a piecewise-constant composition with the density read at each slab's midpoint---the algorithm a closed-form slab code runs. The two are the same expression, as the lower right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile} confirms numerically. Over twenty-seven slab counts spanning four decades, the order-two Magnus method and an independently written midpoint slab product return probability matrices agreeing to between $10^{-15}$ and $10^{-13}$. That is round-off, as the agreement of two evaluations of one formula should be, rather than the agreement of two methods.

A closed-form slab code applied to a continuously varying profile is already doing Magnus at order two without calling it that. Its second-order convergence in the slab width (halving the width buys a factor of four) is the order-two truncation error, not an artifact of freezing the density. What \magnus\ contributes on the identical slab chain is the higher orders, whose errors fall by sixteen, sixty-four, and more per halving. At $1\,024$ slabs of the lower right panel of \figu{phase_vs_profile}, order two stands at $4 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and order four at $10^{-10}$, while order six has already reached $4 \cdot 10^{-15}$. Order two does not reach $10^{-8}$ anywhere on the panel; order six is below it by $128$ slabs. It contributes, separately, the flavor counts and Hamiltonians for which no closed form exists to freeze.

Above order six the commutator-free Gauss--Legendre schemes run out, so we evaluate orders eight and ten on Simpson quadrature instead. That path samples the slab symmetrically about its midpoint, which cancels the odd term of the error expansion and earns two orders beyond the label: order eight converges as $N^{-10}$ and order ten as $N^{-12}$. The gain belongs to the quadrature rather than to the higher orders. Order six run on both paths shows it plainly, falling as $N^{-6}$ on Gauss--Legendre and as $N^{-8}$ on Simpson.

Two errors compete along each curve in that panel. The truncation error falls as $N^{-p}$ at order $p$, while the round-off of the composition grows with $N$, so their sum passes through a minimum and then flattens onto a floor the panel shows as a plateau. Order two is the exception: its truncation error is still the larger of the two at $16\,384$ slabs, so it falls as $N^{-2}$ across the whole panel and reaches $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$ without ever turning. The others meet their minimum sooner the higher the order (at $8\,192$ slabs for order four, $1\,448$ for order six on Gauss--Legendre, $724$ for order six on Simpson, and $362$ for orders eight and ten) and settle onto a common floor near $7 \cdot 10^{-14}$.

The floor belongs to no order. Composing $N$ slabs multiplies $N$ double-precision unitary factors, each carrying a relative error of order $\varepsilon$; those accumulate incoherently. We isolate the multiplication alone by composing the \emph{same} slab operators once in double precision and once at fifty digits, so that the truncation cancels exactly: the difference is $1.5 \cdot 10^{-16}$ at two slabs and $1.6 \cdot 10^{-14}$ at $16\,384$, a growth close to $\sqrt{N}$. The floor the curves reach sits a few times above that, because each factor is itself formed in double precision before it is multiplied in. The panel shows the same thing directly: beyond about a thousand slabs, orders six, eight and ten on Simpson return \emph{identical} numbers at every plotted point. Refining past the minimum costs accuracy rather than buying it, so the useful range of $N$ ends where the plateau begins.

\subsection{Adaptive refinement, and what a tolerance means}
\label{sec:refinement}

By default we do not leave the slab count to the caller. We compute the probability matrix, grow the number of slabs, recompute, and stop when two successive levels agree within ${\tt atol} + {\tt rtol}\lvert P \rvert$. We call that sequence of increasingly fine grids the \emph{refinement ladder}; the count it settles on is what the call is charged for. Four further devices make it efficient.

\smallskip

\textit{A physics-informed starting count.}---Rather than start from one slab, we seed the ladder from an estimate of the accumulated traceless phase $\lVert \Omega_1 \rVert_2$ over the whole trajectory, aiming for roughly $2\pi$ radians per slab, which puts the Gauss--Legendre method close to converged on its first attempt.

\smallskip

\textit{Warm starts across a scan.}---In an energy scan or an oscillogram, each point's refinement is seeded from the previous point's converged counts rather than reclimbing the ladder.

\smallskip

\textit{Slab edges on the discontinuities.}---The PREM profile\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy} is piecewise smooth with density jumps at ten shell boundaries. A slab straddling one of them degrades locally to low order no matter how high the truncation order is set. The Earth entry points solve a quadratic in the arrival direction for the exact chord positions of every boundary crossing and insert them as mandatory slab edges at every refinement level. A caller with a jump of their own (a shock front, say, or a tabulated profile with a kink) passes its position through {\tt t\_breakpoints} and gets the same treatment. Measured over a population of piecewise-constant profiles, declaring the edges moves the median error from $7.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$ to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-12}$.

\smallskip

\textit{A caller-supplied floor.}---Passing a slab count together with a tolerance sets a lower bound on the ladder rather than replacing it, so knowledge about the profile can be added without giving up the refinement, which continues upward from the floor the caller set rather than starting again from a single slab.

\smallskip

\textbf{The tolerances are a stopping criterion, not an error bound.} Nothing in the loop estimates the error of the answer it returns; convergence is \emph{inferred} from agreement between successive levels. Usually that is conservative---for a sequence converging as $C N^{-p}$ the level-to-level gap overstates the error of the finer level---but agreement is evidence, not proof. On a sawtooth density, two successive levels agreed with each other and the returned answer was wrong by $0.855$ in probability. A {\tt strict\_convergence} option requires two \emph{consecutive} agreements for that reason.

Richardson extrapolation would convert that gap into an error estimate, as {\tt NuOscProbExact} does with its slab count. The observed convergence order is not stable enough to divide by. Fitting it on an Earth chord against a $4096$-slab reference gives $p = 3.84$, $5.62$ and $4.06$ at $1$, $2$ and $10$~GeV for a nominal order of four, and $1.15$, $2.66$ and $1.59$ for a nominal order of two---scattered by more than a factor of two, one sequence not even monotone. Assuming $p$ equals the requested order would divide the gap by too large a denominator wherever the true order is lower, reporting an error \emph{smaller} than the truth. Reporting too small an error is the dangerous direction to be wrong in, so we decline to report one at all.

Where a refinement cap is reached before the tolerance is met, \magnus\ returns its best estimate and raises a warning unconditionally, naming how far from converged it stopped as a multiple of the tolerance. The returned probabilities remain exactly unitary, so they can look entirely plausible while being inaccurate---which is the reason the warning is unconditional. Section \ref{sec:diagnostics} gives the measured true- and false-positive rate of every warning the library raises.

\subsection{The matrix exponential}
\label{sec:exponential}

Every slab ends in an exponential, whose cost at these sizes is not incidental. {\tt numpy.linalg.eigh} costs about $1.27~\mu$s per $3\times3$ whatever the stack size---measured $1.268~\mu$s at $N = 108$ and $1.279~\mu$s at $N = 4096$, flat, because it loops over LAPACK internally rather than vectorizing over the stack. We therefore carry a compiled Cayley--Hamilton kernel for $2\times2$ and $3\times3$, which applies to $\mathbb{K}$ the polynomial interpolating $e^{-i\lambda}$ on its spectrum: no eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues in closed form. Interleaved against {\tt eigh} with a control the change cannot touch, the kernel is $6.8$ times faster on the exponential alone at $N = 108$ and $6.9$ at $N = 4096$ ($13.2$ at two flavors), and $2.11$ times faster end to end on a $60$-energy PREM scan.

The gap between $6.8$ and $2.11$ is Amdahl's law rather than a disappointment: the exponential is roughly a third of a slab pass, so removing six sevenths of a third is about what the end-to-end number shows. Quoting the $6.8$ as a package speed-up would be quoting the wrong number. Dimensions four and five keep {\tt eigh} and always will, there being no practical closed form for a $4\times4$ or $5\times5$ Hermitian eigenproblem.

One correction we make in public because it was missed once. The closed form was first verified against random spectra at many norms, and separately against many eigenvalue separations at norm near one. Where those two conditions hold \emph{together} it reached $2.7 \cdot 10^{-7}$ against {\tt eigh}'s $3.0 \cdot 10^{-11}$ (a factor of $7440$) because $\arccos$ has infinite derivative at $u = \pm 1$. Neither single-axis sweep visits that corner. The kernel now hands such matrices to {\tt eigh}; the worst absolute error over the whole separation-by-scale grid is $8.7 \cdot 10^{-14}$; the fraction of matrices declined on real work---PREM chords, solar slab chains, constant density, non-standard interactions---measures $0.00\%$. Switching backend moves probabilities by at most $4.6 \cdot 10^{-15}$ across all of those.

\subsection{Adiabatic transport with exact Magnus patches}
\label{sec:hybrid}

Section \ref{sec:phase_vs_profile} showed the cost growing slowly with accumulated phase, not vanishing. For a few-MeV solar neutrino crossing most of the Sun, $\Phi$ reaches $10^{3}$--$10^{6}$ radians and the slab ladder can exhaust its cap before the tolerance is met. Physically, though, nothing there is happening quickly: away from a level crossing the flavor content follows the instantaneous eigenstates of $\mathbb{H}$ adiabatically, which is exactly what the Mikheyev--Smirnov--Wolfenstein description of solar neutrinos says\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1977ue, Mikheev:1986gs, Parke:1986jy, Bethe:1986ej, Kuo:1989qe}.

\magnus\ automates that. Diagonalizing $\mathbb{H}(l) = \mathbb{W}(l)\,{\rm diag}(\lambda_k)\,\mathbb{W}^\dagger(l)$ at each position, adiabatic transport is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:adiabatic}
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm ad}(l_1, l_0)
 =
 \mathbb{W}(l_1)\,{\rm diag}\!\left(e^{-i\Phi_k(l_1)}\right)\mathbb{W}^\dagger(l_0) \;,
\end{equation}
with $\Phi_k = \int \lambda_k\,ds$ the dynamical phase, integrated by Simpson's rule over a grid of diagonalized Hamiltonians. It never resolves the accumulated phase step by step, because $\Phi_k$ is a smooth integral however large it becomes. The geometric (Berry) phase needs no separate formula: at each grid step the columns of $\mathbb{W}$ are phase-fixed by maximizing the real, positive overlap with the previous step's, a discrete parallel transport that captures it implicitly, and correctly for genuinely complex Hamiltonians. The result is exactly unitary (a diagonal phase conjugated by unitary matrices) independently of how fine the grid is, so refining it buys accuracy without ever costing unitarity, a property no refinement level can trade away.

Where adiabaticity fails, we repair it rather than approximate it. Non-adiabatic windows are located with the Hellmann--Feynman identities\ \cite{Feynman:1939zza}
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:hf}
 \frac{d\lambda_k}{dl} = \langle v_k \lvert \tfrac{d\mathbb{H}}{dl} \rvert v_k \rangle ,
 \;\;
 \gamma_{jk} = \frac{\lvert\langle v_j \lvert \tfrac{d\mathbb{H}}{dl} \rvert v_k \rangle\rvert}
 {(\lambda_k - \lambda_j)^2} \;,
\end{equation}
which need only the derivative of the smooth, gauge-independent Hamiltonian and never that of an eigenvector (defined only up to a phase). Every pair $(j,k)$ is scanned, at any dimension, so no number of simultaneous resonances defeats the search. A window is grown outward from each candidate until $\gamma_{jk}$ falls back below threshold, which makes it a physical width rather than an artifact of the search grid. Overlapping windows are merged. Inside each we compute the evolution operator with the package's own Magnus kernel, refined until two levels agree.

We then compose the pieces by the exact law
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:hybrid_composition}
 \mathbb{U}
 =
 \mathbb{U}_{\rm ad}\,\mathbb{U}_{\rm patch}\, \cdots \,\mathbb{U}_{\rm ad} \;,
\end{equation}
which holds regardless of which method computed each factor, and is unitary because each factor is. The crossing is therefore treated exactly rather than by a Landau--Zener formula\ \cite{Zener:1932ws}. As a check, the computed hop probability reproduces the analytic Landau--Zener value to a few parts in a thousand for a linear crossing. Nothing in the implementation assumes that formula, so the agreement tests the crossing rather than restating an expression the code never evaluates.

None of the thresholds involved has a value that is safe for an arbitrary Hamiltonian, so the strategy never trusts a single evaluation: it repeats the whole computation with the threshold tightened and both grids doubled, and reports the result as \emph{certified} only once two successive levels agree. Failing that, it returns its best estimate (still exactly unitary) and says it is not certified. Against a tight {\tt DOP853} ground truth across a validation grid spanning zero, one, two separated and two merged resonances, at three, four and five flavors, real and CP-violating, the strategy is $30$ to $4800$ times faster with a maximum absolute error of $2.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and unitarity between $10^{-16}$ and $10^{-12}$. Both blind spots are properties of a fixed grid rather than of this implementation; Section \ref{sec:limitations} gives the measurement behind each.

\subsection{The phase-averaged limit, in closed form}
\label{sec:averaged}

A neutrino from an astrophysical source arrives with a phase nobody resolves. For a TeV neutrino from $100$~Mpc the accumulated phase is of order $10^{15}$ radians; no ingredient of that number (the distance, the size of the production region, the detector's energy resolution) is known to anything approaching the precision the phase would demand. Whatever the true phase, the measurement integrates over many complete cycles of it. Propagating such a probability is therefore both expensive and beside the point.

Writing the amplitude in the eigenbasis of $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{V}\,{\rm diag}(\lambda_i)\,\mathbb{V}^\dagger$ and averaging over the phase kills every interference term, leaving
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_i \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}\rvert^2\, \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\beta i}\rvert^2 \;.
\end{equation}
This is not an approximation to be refined: it is the exact $L/E \to \infty$ limit, costing one matrix product rather than an integration. Three consequences follow immediately. It is symmetric in $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$; it is identical for neutrinos and antineutrinos, since $\lvert \mathbb{V}^\ast \rvert^2 = \lvert \mathbb{V} \rvert^2$, so CP violation does not survive the average even though $\delta_{\rm CP}$ still enters through the magnitudes; and in vacuum it depends on neither energy nor baseline, since scaling $\mathbb{H}$ by $1/E$ leaves its eigenvectors alone.

Coherence is a physical question, and we treat it as one. Equation (\ref{equ:averaged}) assumes every \emph{relative} phase has averaged away, which is a statement about pairs of eigenvalues rather than about the spectrum as a whole. Two eigenvalues close enough to keep their relative phase fixed stay coherent and their cross term survives, so the implementation groups the spectrum into blocks of mutually coherent eigenvalues and sums coherently inside each,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged_blocks}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_b \Big\lvert \sum_{i \in b} \mathbb{V}^\ast_{\alpha i}\mathbb{V}_{\beta i} \Big\rvert^2 \;,
\end{equation}
which reduces to \equ{averaged} when every block is a singleton. The distinction is not academic: a sterile state with a small $\Delta m^2_{41}$, or an exactly degenerate spectrum, makes the naive sum quietly wrong---with all eigenvalues equal the correct answer is the identity, nothing having oscillated, while \equ{averaged} returns a spurious mixture. Where a pair sits between the two limits, neither expression describes the result. The library says so rather than returning a number the physics does not support.

Where the Hamiltonian varies there is no single eigenbasis to decohere in. A neutrino produced at $l_0$ decoheres in the eigenbasis \emph{there}, is carried along the levels, and is detected in the eigenbasis at $l_1$:
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:averaged_varying}
 \left\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} \right\rangle
 =
 \sum_{ij} \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}(l_0)\rvert^2\,
 P^{\rm cross}_{ij}\,
 \lvert \mathbb{V}_{\beta j}(l_1)\rvert^2 \;,
\end{equation}
the standard MSW-plus-decoherence result, generalized here to any number of levels and any number of crossings. $P^{\rm cross}$ is the identity wherever adiabaticity holds, and departs from it only across a non-adiabatic window, whose transfer matrix is computed with the convergence-checked Magnus patch of Section \ref{sec:hybrid}---exactly, rather than by an asymptotic formula.

We show what this is worth on the Sun in Section \ref{sec:solar}, and why estimating the same quantity by averaging a scan does not converge in Section \ref{sec:averaging_is_not_estimating}.

\subsection{The six engines}
\label{sec:engines}

The sections above describe six ways of computing a probability, and a caller asks for none of them by name. Figure~\ref{fig:strategies} shows all six and the order in which they are tried. Each row draws what its engine does to the trajectory: one exponential for the closed-form average of Section \ref{sec:averaged}; adiabatic transport with an exact Magnus patch across the resonance, from Section \ref{sec:hybrid}; the fast vacuum phase removed analytically before a single Magnus pass; one evaluation of the profile shared across many energies; one pass returning many baselines; and the general ladder of Section \ref{sec:refinement}, refining until two successive stages agree. In the order they are tried:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \emph{Closed-form average.} Evaluates $\langle P \rangle$ directly from the eigenvectors of a position-independent $\mathbb{H}$, propagating nothing (Section \ref{sec:averaged}).
 \item \emph{Adiabatic $+$ Magnus.} Carries the state along the instantaneous eigenbasis and stitches an exact Magnus patch across each resonance, so the accumulated phase costs nothing away from a crossing (Section \ref{sec:hybrid}).
 \item \emph{Interaction picture.} Removes the fast vacuum phase analytically and takes one Magnus pass over what is left; two flavors, on a profile tagged as exponential.
 \item \emph{Energy-batched scan.} Builds the profile once and shares it across every energy of a scan, rather than rebuilding it per point.
 \item \emph{Cumulative scan.} Walks the trajectory once and returns the probability at every baseline along it.
 \item \emph{General Magnus ladder.} The slab chain of Section \ref{sec:refinement}, refined until two successive stages agree. It is never skipped.
\end{enumerate}
Table~\ref{tab:engines} gives the condition under which each is taken.

None is a special case of another. Each declines requests it cannot serve honestly and falls through to the next, and the last accepts whatever reaches it. The fallbacks are silent by design, because they happen on ordinary calls and warning about them would be noise. Any call can be asked which engine answered it, whether the hybrid strategy certified, and which engines declined and why---which is the answer to ``why did my result move?'' and ``why did this call get slow?'', neither of which was answerable from outside the package before.

The grouping matters more than the list, because a cross-check between two engines is worth something only if they can fail differently. The general ladder, the cumulative baseline scan and the energy-batched scan all walk slabs with the same kernel and are one family. The two-flavor interaction-picture path factors the fast vacuum phase out analytically first, so what it must resolve is a different function. The hybrid strategy is a genuinely different method, whose blind spots are the resonance detector's rather than the quadrature's. And {\tt scipy.linalg.expm} is independent of all of them, but is exact only where the Hamiltonian is constant or declared piecewise constant. Two engines in the same family can be wrong in the same way at the same time: their disagreement is informative, their agreement is not.

\subsection{Scope and interface}
\label{sec:scope_interface}

\magnus\ is written entirely in Python and requires version 3.10 or later. Its dependencies ({\tt NumPy}\ \cite{Harris:2020xlr}, {\tt SciPy}\ \cite{Virtanen:2019joe}, {\tt joblib}, {\tt Numba}\ \cite{Lam:2015numba} and {\tt Matplotlib}\ \cite{Hunter:2007ouj}) are installed with it. We import {\tt Matplotlib} lazily inside the plotting helpers, so {\tt import magnus} does not pay for it.

The primordial entry point is {\tt osc\_prob}. Its argument is a Hamiltonian: any callable ${\tt H\_func}(l)$ returning a Hermitian matrix of any size, or a constant matrix. Everything else in the public interface builds one. Around it we provide some sixty explicitly named wrappers, covering every combination of flavor count ($2$, $3$, $4$, $5$), environment (vacuum, constant density, exponential density, the Earth through PREM, the Sun) and scenario (standard, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation). A user working on a standard case then passes named parameters ({\tt s12}, {\tt eps\_em}, {\tt rho}) rather than assembling dictionaries. \ref{app:structure} describes the three layers this is organized into and the contract between them.

Every entry point takes arrays. An energy scan, a set of baselines, or an oscillogram in energy and arrival direction is one call, and we recognize several structures that make a batch cheaper than its parts. When the Hamiltonian separates as $\mathbb{H}(E, l) = \mathbb{H}_E(E) + V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}$ (which the standard, non-standard-interaction and Lorentz-violating Hamiltonians all do), we sample the potential once per refinement level and share it across every energy. The quadrature, the commutator algebra, the exponentials and the slab products then carry the energy axis as a batch dimension, and per-energy convergence masking stops recomputing the energies that have already converged. When the potential does not vary at all, the series terminates at its first term and a whole scan is one stacked exponential. When many baselines share one energy, each is a prefix of the longest and a single traversal answers all of them. Section \ref{sec:engines} lists the six engines and the order they are tried in. Any call can be asked which one answered it.

\subsection{Code validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The repository carries $1207$ tests, run on Python $3.10$ through $3.13$, covering $93\%$ of the library, and including a clean-room installation of the built wheel. \ref{app:tests} groups them by what they cover. The validation is layered, its layers independent on purpose.

\smallskip

\textit{Against the derivation.}---We compare the terms $\Omega_1, \ldots, \Omega_6$ term by term against an independently coded implementation of the Bernoulli recursion, on a Hamiltonian with three independent non-commuting generators, chosen because a two-generator Hamiltonian makes one nested-commutator term of $\Omega_4$ vanish identically and would mask a coefficient error. Agreement is at machine precision for every order from one to ten.

\smallskip

\textit{Against closed forms.}---Two- and three-flavor vacuum oscillations and two-flavor constant-density matter oscillations, for neutrinos and antineutrinos, are checked against their analytic expressions to machine precision.

\smallskip

\textit{Against an independent solver.}---A tight {\tt DOP853} integration\ \cite{Prince:1981dop} at ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-12}$, ${\tt atol} = 10^{-14}$ is the accuracy oracle, and its own convergence is verified per configuration by tightening to $10^{-13}$ and confirming that the movement is far below the error being quoted. Where the profile makes {\tt scipy.linalg.expm} exact---a constant or declared piecewise-constant Hamiltonian---that is used instead, because it is not an approximation at all. The measured convergence rates under slab halving are $4.0$, $16.0$ and $63.8$ at orders two, four and six, matching $2^{\rm order}$.

\smallskip

\textit{Against itself, where that is meaningful.}---The energy-batched scan is asserted \emph{bit-identical} to the per-point path, and a parallel run bit-identical to a serial one, so an optimization that changed an answer fails rather than passes quietly. No Magnus path is ever scored against another as an accuracy measurement; a cross-check between two paths is reported as agreement and never as accuracy.

\smallskip

\textit{Statistically.}---We fuzz. A suite asserts on the \emph{distribution} of the error over populations of random profiles---silent-miss rate, median, worst case---rather than on individual cases, because a per-case assertion on random input is brittle while an aggregate one still catches a regression that moves the distribution. Over $145$ random smooth profiles at two through five flavors the median error is $6.1 \cdot 10^{-8}$ with $4.1\%$ silent misses; over $150$ random piecewise-constant profiles with their edges declared it is $1.3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ with none; over $164$ Earth and solar configurations, none. A \emph{silent miss} is an answer outside the requested tolerance carrying no warning of any kind. It is the only failure mode that matters: an inaccurate answer that says so is the warnings' job.

We run all of it in continuous integration on every push, across the four supported Python versions, together with a linter, a documentation build with warnings as errors, and the execution of every notebook, so that an example which stops working cannot survive a merge.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Limitations are of two kinds. Those of the method are assumptions no better programming would remove; those of the implementation are contingent on choices made in the code.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsubsection{Limitations of the method}

\textit{Open systems.}---\magnus\ solves the unitary Schr\"odinger equation for a Hermitian Hamiltonian; every truncation of the series lives in the Lie algebra, so the package is architecturally committed to norm-preserving evolution. Quantum decoherence, coupling to a bath, and neutrino decay to invisible states all remove probability from the system or damp coherence between mass eigenstates, and require a density matrix under a non-unitary master equation. A Hermitian effective Hamiltonian cannot encode a decay width---that needs an anti-Hermitian term, which breaks the very property the method rests on. {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} is the tool for those.

\smallskip

\textit{Collective oscillations.}---In a dense environment the effective Hamiltonian depends on the neutrino flavor content itself\ \cite{Duan:2010bg, Mirizzi:2015eza}, which is a nonlinear, self-consistent problem. \magnus\ assumes $\mathbb{H}$ is a \emph{known} function of energy and position supplied by the caller, not a functional of the solution.

\smallskip

\textit{A constant Hamiltonian in a hurry.}---Where the Hamiltonian does not change, an exact algebraic solution beats a truncated series. That is arithmetic rather than a defect in anybody's code. Section \ref{sec:comparison} measures how much it is worth.

\smallskip

\textit{A feature narrower than any grid.}---Every diagnostic here samples the Hamiltonian on some grid. A feature narrower than the finest of them is invisible to all of them at once. Measured on a Gaussian resonance of width $10^{-5}$ of the trajectory, every engine reported a smooth profile, a small adiabaticity parameter, and a resolved Hamiltonian (correctly, given what any of them can see) and all were wrong together by $2.9 \cdot 10^{-2}$ against a requested $10^{-3}$. Because they are wrong \emph{together}, a cross-check between engines cannot see it either: disagreement is what a cross-check detects, so it finds a wrong engine exactly when some other engine got it right. The cure is a caller-supplied breakpoint at the feature, which takes the same case to $8.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$. This is a property of any fixed grid, not of any particular test.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsubsection{Limitations of the implementation}
\label{sec:implementation_limits}

\textit{The sterile matter potential takes one scalar per trajectory.}---The matter term factorizes as $V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}$, and $\mathbb{P}$ carries the sterile states' entry through the neutron-to-proton ratio. The density therefore varies per PREM layer while $\mathbb{P}$ does not, so on an Earth chord with a sterile state a single composition is used for a path that crosses iron and rock. No single value is right for such a chord. \magnus\ detects the case: it warns when the layer values disagree by more than $2\%$ and names the path-averaged value to pass; no number changes unless the caller acts. A per-layer projector is the fix and is tracked as an open issue; the Sun has the same shape and is worse, its composition varying more strongly with radius.

\smallskip

\textit{Broadband roughness is invisible to the structural tests.}---The sub-probe feature scan is a \emph{concentration} statistic, which is what makes it blind to structure spread evenly over every scale rather than piled into one place. We measure it on Kolmogorov density fluctuations built the way the supernova literature builds them: a $k^{-5/3}$ spectrum with a $40$--$50$~dB dynamic range, so that there is power below every grid the package lays down. On those, the finest reachable grid sees only a third of the profile's total variation. Neither structural test detects it on any of the $144$ configurations. What saves the answer there is unrelated machinery: the errors such a profile produces are caught by the \emph{convergence} checks, which watch the refinement ladder rather than the profile. The outcome is correct (the caller is warned) but by accident of mechanism. On a turbulent or noisy medium, treat the structural diagnostics as silent by construction and rely on the tolerance machinery, or supply breakpoints.

\smallskip

\textit{The hybrid strategy needs a smooth Hamiltonian.}---Its diagnostics finite-difference $\mathbb{H}(l)$, which is meaningless across a genuine discontinuity, so a profile with declared breakpoints (an Earth chord, always) falls back to the general path unconditionally. That is the principled consequence of the smoothness requirement rather than a special case written in.

\smallskip

\textit{Accuracy at an engine boundary.}---Six internal engines can answer a request, listed in Section \ref{sec:engines}; the point at which one hands over to another we call a \emph{seam}. The accuracy steps across a seam rather than varying smoothly, because the engines on either side are different methods. Adding one baseline to a scan just below the seam changes the answer---by as much as five orders of magnitude in accuracy, and always \emph{toward} the truth. It is a documentation matter rather than a numerical one, but a user scanning the number of points and watching their answer move will otherwise assume something is broken.

For \magnus\ it is a mixed one: the PREM profile\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy} varies smoothly \emph{within} each of its ten shells and jumps \emph{between} them, so it exercises both halves of Section \ref{sec:refinement}. The {\tt earth} module solves the chord geometry, finds the exact positions at which a trajectory crosses each shell boundary, and hands them to the refinement as mandatory slab edges. Notebook \#6 builds oscillograms like these from scratch, and notebook \#4 treats the long-baseline case they generalize.

One convention goes with it and is not a default the caller cannot reach. PREM is a density model and fixes no composition, so the electron fraction is supplied separately. \magnus\ resolves it per PREM layer (about $0.4656$ for the iron core and $0.4957$ for the mantle) which differs from a uniform $0.5$ by up to $7 \cdot 10^{-2}$ in $P_{\nu_\mu \to \nu_e}$ on a core-crossing chord. That is far above anything a solver comparison can resolve, which is why every cross-code measurement in Section \ref{sec:comparison} passes the composition explicitly on both sides rather than leaving it to a default.

\subsubsection{Averaging a scan is not the same as asking for the average}
\label{sec:averaging_is_not_estimating}

The tempting route to the averaged quantity is to compute the instantaneous probability across an energy window and take the mean. It does not converge.

A finite-window mean is an estimator with a bias of its own, and on a profile whose density varies across the window that bias does not shrink as the window widens---because a wider window also averages over different matter conditions. Measured on the solar ray above, the window mean moves from $0.5924$ to $0.6023$ between six and forty-eight oscillation lengths, drifting \emph{away} from rather than toward a limit. There is no width at which one can stop and claim convergence. Asked for the limit instead, \magnus\ reproduces the analytic adiabatic MSW expression $\langle P_{ee}\rangle = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\theta_m(l_0)\cos2\theta_m(l_1)$ to $3 \cdot 10^{-16}$ across $1$--$20$~MeV, and matches it to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-5}$ on the full tabulated model where the analytic expression is itself an idealization. Forty averaged energies take about $0.7$~s.


The obvious guard does not catch the related trap. \magnus\ reports, on request, how coarsely a scan samples the oscillation it is computing---the number of cycles per step, and how many points a Nyquist-adequate scan would need. On the trajectories this package serves those numbers are stark: an Earth chord is about $430$ oscillations long and would need $861$ baselines, a solar ray about $2200$ and $4390$, a supernova ray about $37\,000$ and $73\,392$. The measurement is \emph{reported and never warned about}, deliberately: the Nyquist criterion is objectively correct and would fire on $44$ of $45$ realistic scan sizes, and a warning firing on $98\%$ of calls is noise however right each firing is---and it would teach users to silence a category that also carries genuine discontinuity warnings.

At a front width of $0.07$~km each jump is, on the scale of the oscillation length, a discontinuity: the probability changes character across a single point. Widened to $70$~km, nothing else altered, the same jump becomes a smooth function spanning half the window and the transition is gradual. We widen both jumps together, which is why the two columns already differ below the forward shock and not only at it. 

The oscillation length that matters here is the local one. In vacuum at $15$~MeV it is some $500$~km, which is what makes the ray $140$ of them long, but at the density of the contact discontinuity the matter potential shortens it to $16$~km. A $0.07$~km ramp is therefore crossed faster than the state can respond, which projects it onto the new eigenbasis; a $70$~km one is crossed slowly enough that the state follows the instantaneous eigenstate instead. What differs between the columns is therefore the conversion probability itself rather than the phase of an oscillation.

The wider contact ramp begins at $12\,313$~km. Past it the two columns differ by $0.22$ on average through the shocked region, the wider front leaving a mean survival of $0.91$ against $0.72$. Sweeping the width confirms both halves of that statement: the columns stay together for every ramp thinner than the local oscillation length, and part once it is reached. The width at which they part follows that length as we vary the energy, at $6.3$~km for $5$~MeV, $15.7$~km for $15$~MeV and $46.6$~km for $45$~MeV, which is the signature of a change in adiabaticity rather than of a resolution effect.

Away from the two transitions the profiles are identical to the last bit, so nothing else in the figure can be responsible. The four scenarios drawn differ only in the Hermitian matrix handed to the same call. Two flavors barely oscillate here, since only $\Delta m^2_{21}$ is available to them at this energy; the others convert strongly, and the sterile system most of all.

The lesson generalizes past this example. The question to ask of a profile is not what physics is in it but what it looks like at the scale of the oscillation length. A staircase belongs to a closed form and a smooth function to an integrator, \ie, the profile decides, and the same physical object can be either, depending on how well it is resolved. Section \ref{sec:shock_comparison} puts numbers to both halves of that.

\subsection{High-energy astrophysical neutrinos}
\label{sec:astro}

Figure~\ref{fig:astro} shows the flavor composition that a TeV--PeV astrophysical flux arrives with at Earth, and the average flavor-transition probabilities behind it, for four Hamiltonians over $1$~TeV to $10$~PeV. The top row carries the survival probabilities $\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha}\rangle$; the bottom row carries the flavor fractions reaching Earth from a source that produces neutrinos through the full pion decay chain. The standard three-flavor case is repeated beneath each panel so that a departure from it is legible, and the dotted line in the second column marks the energy at which the Lorentz-violating term equals the vacuum one.

\textit{Producing them.}---In cosmic accelerators---active galaxies, gamma-ray bursts, supernovae---protons interact with ambient matter and radiation to produce pions\ \cite{Margolis:1977wt, Stecker:1978ah, Kelner:2006tc}. The pions decay through $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + \nu_\mu$, followed by $\mu^+ \to \bar{\nu}_\mu + \nu_e + e^+$, and their charge conjugates, so that the flux leaving the source carries flavor ratios $(f_e, f_\mu, f_\tau)_{\rm S} = \left(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, 0\right)$, with $f_{\alpha, {\rm S}}$ the fraction of $\nu_\alpha + \bar{\nu}_\alpha$ in the total. Neutrino telescopes cannot ordinarily tell $\nu$ from $\bar\nu$, so $\nu_\alpha$ below means $\nu_\alpha + \bar{\nu}_\alpha$. We take that full pion-decay composition throughout. Notebook \#10 develops the averaged limit that this section rests on, and notebook \#23 says when averaging a scan is not the same as asking for the average.

\textit{What is observable.}---Over Mpc--Gpc baselines the oscillation length is minute against the distance, and neither the distance, the size of the production region, nor the energy resolution of a detector is known to anything approaching the precision the phase would demand. A telescope is therefore sensitive only to the average flavor-transition probabilities $\langle P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}\rangle$ of \equ{averaged}, which \magnus\ returns in closed form from the eigenvectors of $\mathbb{H}$ rather than by propagating a phase it is about to average away. In vacuum $\mathbb{V}$ is the PMNS matrix and \equ{averaged} is the familiar $\sum_i \lvert U_{\alpha i}\rvert^2 \lvert U_{\beta i}\rvert^2$; with matter, a sterile state or a new term in $\mathbb{H}$ it is the same expression evaluated in whatever eigenbasis that Hamiltonian has. The composition at Earth follows as $f_{\alpha, \oplus} = \sum_\beta \langle P_{\nu_\beta \to \nu_\alpha}\rangle f_{\beta, {\rm S}}$. Listing \ref{lst:astro} is both steps.

\textit{What varies with energy, and what does not.}---For standard oscillations the composition does not depend on the energy at all. Equation~(\ref{equ:averaged}) is built from $\lvert \mathbb{V}_{\alpha i}\rvert^2$ alone, and in vacuum no energy enters it. Two of the three departures we draw are flat for the same reason. A sterile state moves the composition from $0.326:0.343:0.330$ to $0.330:0.325:0.346$ and leaves it there, after we renormalize past the $13.3\%$ of the flux it removes. Non-standard interactions through the Earth move it by less than $0.005$ and likewise do not vary, because above a TeV the matter term dominates the Hamiltonian inside the Earth and carries no energy dependence of its own. Both are shifts to a different constant.

Only a term that grows with the energy leaves a signature that varies with it. The Lorentz-violating case carries $\nu_e$ from $0.326$ up to $0.348$ and down again to $0.317$ across the crossover, with $\nu_\mu$ dipping to $0.328$ and recovering; the survival probability driving it reaches $0.894$. That is what a flavor measurement across a decade of energy could distinguish, and it is the case for being able to evaluate \equ{averaged} for a Hamiltonian nobody has written down.

\subsection{A Hamiltonian of your own}
\label{sec:arbitrary}

Everything above goes through named wrappers, none of which is privileged. Listing \ref{lst:arbitrary} passes a Hamiltonian the package knows nothing about, which is the escape hatch and also the design: the wrappers build a callable and hand it to {\tt osc\_prob}, and a caller can skip the first step.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={\magnus\ with an arbitrary, user-supplied Hamiltonian, here the long-range $L_e - L_\mu$ interaction of \equ{h3_lri} sourced by the electrons of the Sun. The profile and $V_{\rm CC}$ are the solar model of \figu{solar}; only $V_{e\mu}$ is new, and what it costs the caller is the last two lines. The {\tt [..., None, None]} is what lets one call evaluate the Hamiltonian at every quadrature node of every slab at once; written to take a scalar, the same function returns bit-identical numbers $4.6$ times slower.}, label={lst:arbitrary}, float=tbp]
import numpy as np
import magnus.avgprob as avgprob
import magnus.globaldefs as gd

h_vac = hamiltonians.\
 hamiltonian_3nu_vacuum_energy_independent(**osc)
e00 = np.diag([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])

# V_emu [eV] on the solar radial grid, from the
# Yukawa integral of Eq. (20) over the same n_e(r)
# the matter term reads, so the two potentials
# cannot disagree about the electron distribution
v_emu = g2*long_range_potential(r, n_e, 1.0/R_SUN)

# nu_e and nu_mu carry opposite charge, nu_tau none
q = np.diag([1.0, -1.0, 0.0])

def H_func(l):
 VCC = np.asarray(vcc_sun(l))
 return (h_vac/energy
 + VCC[..., None, None]*e00

\end{lstlisting}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Two properties govern how one should be written. The engine samples the Hamiltonian at every quadrature node of every slab (a few hundred positions for a single probability, repeated at each refinement level) so a function that accepts only one position at a time turns that into a Python loop. \magnus\ probes the function once, verifies a vectorized evaluation against a scalar spot-check, and uses whichever form works; the fallback is correct and $4.6$ times slower, with bit-identical output, and since v1.0.0 it says so once per session and names the fix. The {\tt [..., None, None]} in Listing \ref{lst:arbitrary} is the whole of that fix.

Figure~\ref{fig:solar_lri} shows what that buys, on the solar ray of Section \ref{sec:sun}. \emph{Top}: the phase-averaged survival probability $\langle P_{\nu_e \to \nu_e}\rangle$ against energy, for the standard three-flavor Hamiltonian and for two versions of a new one, at mediator ranges of $R_\odot$ and a tenth of it. \emph{Bottom}: the difference each makes. Nothing in the package anticipates the new term, and nothing needs to: the same averaging machinery of Section \ref{sec:averaged} answers, because it never asked what the Hamiltonian contained.

\textit{The interaction.}---The example is a long-range neutrino-electron interaction, made possible by a new light neutral gauge boson $Z^\prime$ from gauging the anomaly-free combination $L_e - L_\mu$\ \cite{He:1990pn, Foot:1994vd}, with $L_e$ and $L_\mu$ the electron and muon lepton numbers. The charge under it, $Q \equiv L_e - L_\mu$, is $+1$ for the electron and $\nu_e$, $-1$ for the muon and $\nu_\mu$, and zero for every other Standard-Model field. A new matter potential sourced by electrons therefore enters the Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_lri}
 \mathbb{H}^{\rm LRI}(E, l)
 =
 \frac{\mathbb{A}}{E} + V_{\rm CC}(l)\,\mathbb{P}
 + V_{e\mu}(l)\,{\rm diag}\left(1, -1, 0\right) \;,
\end{equation}
where the first two terms are the vacuum and charged-current matter terms of Section \ref{sec:recap} and the third is the whole of the modification.

\textit{A non-local potential.}---Under $L_e - L_\mu$ an electron sources a Yukawa potential centered on its position, so what a neutrino at $\mathbf{r}$ feels is an integral over the electrons around it,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:yukawa}
 V_{e\mu}(\mathbf{r})
 =
 \frac{g^{\prime 2}}{4\pi}
 \int d^3r^\prime \, n_e(\mathbf{r}^\prime) \,
 \frac{e^{-m_{Z^\prime} \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}^\prime \rvert}}
      {\lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}^\prime \rvert} \;,
\end{equation}
with $g^\prime$ the new gauge coupling. Where $V_{\rm CC}$ reads the density at the neutrino's own position, \equ{yukawa} is \emph{non-local}: at mediator masses light enough to matter its value at any radius is an integral over the whole electron distribution, so it depends on how many electrons there are and only weakly on where they sit\ \cite{Wise:2018rnb}. Its variation along a trajectory is a matter of geometry rather than of the local density.

That is what makes the Sun a sharper test of it than the Earth. The solar density falls by five orders of magnitude along the ray, so $V_{\rm CC}$ and $V_{e\mu}$ fall at quite different rates, and the shape of the new potential---not merely its size---is what the observable responds to. We fix the coupling separately for each mediator range so that both put $V_{e\mu}$ at a tenth of $V_{\rm CC}$ at the center; what then differs between the two curves is that shape alone. At half a solar radius the longer-ranged potential still stands at $2.5$ times $V_{\rm CC}$ where the shorter has fallen to $0.4$.

The two shift the averaged probability by up to $0.021$ and $0.018$ across $0.1$--$20$~MeV, and they are not a rescaling of one another: the shorter range samples the profile where it falls fastest, and its shift changes sign at a different energy. Both curves are computed with no expansion in $V_{e\mu}$, because the method does not care what the Hermitian matrix contains. Notebook \#19 develops the potential and checks it against a closed form.

The second is that a Hamiltonian which ignores its argument is detected separately and routed to the constant-Hamiltonian engine, so the easiest case there is does not take the slowest path available. It used to: the dispatcher declined a non-callable potential, so a $60$-energy constant-density scan made $18\,000$ separate calls, each rediscovering the same constancy.

\section{Performance and precision}
\label{sec:performance}

We measured every timing quoted in this paper on one machine, so absolute numbers mean little on their own: read the \emph{ratios} within a row, and read a tolerance sweep only within one code's own curve. ``Code A is faster than code B'' does not survive a change of hardware; ``tightening this dial costs twenty percent and buys four orders of magnitude'' does. Where a measurement compares two versions or two code paths, we take it with a harness that interleaves them round-robin and carries workloads the change cannot touch as controls. Those controls came back at exactly $1.00\times$ while a fuzzer pegged a core, which is what makes the ratios usable at all.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{What a probability costs}
\label{sec:cost}

A single three-flavor Earth probability takes about $2$~ms at the default tolerance. Across $164$ Earth and solar configurations spanning two to five flavors, standard and non-standard Hamiltonians, neutrinos and antineutrinos, the median call is $2$~ms and the slowest is $0.90$~s.

Three things make a scan much faster than its parts, none of them changing any answer.

\smallskip

\textit{Pass arrays.}---Every wrapper takes an array of energies, of baselines, or both. For a position-dependent Hamiltonian we then build the profile once for the whole scan rather than once per point (what the energy-batched engine exists for). Where the potential does not vary at all, the constant-Hamiltonian engine turns the scan into one stacked exponential. Measured against the per-point route it replaces, that is $15.5$--$18.9\times$ at three flavors, $17.3$--$24.7\times$ at two, and $6$--$7.4\times$ at four and five. The last two gain less because they exponentiate through {\tt eigh}. Results are bit-identical to the route they replace at every flavor count and both neutrino signs.

\smallskip

\textit{Make the Hamiltonian array-capable.}---The single largest factor under a caller's control, measured at $4.6\times$ on a three-flavor exponential-density profile with bit-identical output.

\smallskip

\textit{An Earth chord is a palindrome.}---A neutrino crossing a spherically symmetric Earth meets every radius twice, so we evaluate the Hamiltonian on the first half of the slab chain and take the rest by reversal. That halves the calls to the caller's Hamiltonian and nothing else; we leave the exponential and the commutator algebra untouched. It is worth exactly what that Hamiltonian costs: $0.91\times$ for a plain PREM density lookup, too cheap to be worth halving, and $1.41$--$1.67\times$ for an expensive one. We declare the symmetry rather than detect it, because detecting it would need the very evaluations the optimization exists to skip.

The Earth entry points are where that declaration belongs: there, a chord meeting every radius twice is a fact of geometry rather than an assumption about the profile. The symmetry is carried by the interval it applies to rather than set as a global flag. A standard PREM energy scan gains nothing from it, and that is as it should be. The separable engine already evaluates the profile once and shares it across every energy, which is the same saving taken earlier and more completely.

\smallskip

One cost runs the other way. The adaptive ladder computes the probability at several slab counts and stops when two agree, so a call at a tight tolerance pays for the levels below it as well as for the confidence it buys. Passing a slab count directly skips that, at the cost of the evidence it buys.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Precision, accuracy, and diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

Precision and accuracy have separate limits here, as they do in any code. Both are separate again from \emph{unitarity}, which refinement does not reach at all. Precision is internal. The batched and per-point paths, and the parallel and serial ones, agree bit-for-bit, which the test suite asserts as an identity rather than a tolerance. The two matrix-exponential backends agree to about $10^{-15}$, and to $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ on a solar profile that chains $33\,575$ slab exponentials---within the $N\varepsilon = 7.4 \cdot 10^{-12}$ that an ordered product of that length allows.

Accuracy is measured against something outside the library. Section \ref{sec:validation} gives the oracles and the distributions. At the default {\tt rtol} $=$ {\tt atol} $= 10^{-3}$ an Earth crossing comes back accurate to about $5 \cdot 10^{-4}$ against a $10^{-7}$-tolerance reference.

Because the tolerance is a stopping criterion, the warnings matter. We report their measured rates rather than asserting their usefulness. Over $168$ configurations across the profile families the package serves, scored against {\tt DOP853} or---for piecewise-constant profiles, where it is exact---{\tt expm}, the convergence warning fired $70$ times with $17$ true positives, the unmarked-discontinuity warning $56$ times with $23$, and the tolerance-not-achieved warning $37$ times with $16$. Silent misses across that whole population: $2$ of $168$.

Those false-positive rates want reading rather than quoting. The unmarked-discontinuity warning reports a \emph{condition about the input}, not a prediction about the error, and on all $33$ of its false positives the condition was real (there was an undeclared discontinuity) and the answer survived anyway; declaring the edges would still have improved it by orders of magnitude. A warning whose claim is true and whose advice is worth taking is not made a false alarm by the answer surviving.

The convergence warning's $76\%$ has a known cause and a measured \emph{non}-fix: of $66$ single-point calls, some refinement level exceeded the convergence radius in $46$, but the level whose answer was returned did so in only $7$, so keying the warning to the returned level looks obviously right. Implemented and re-measured over the same population, it made the warning worse---firings fell from $70$ to $53$ but true positives fell from $17$ to $4$. ``The ladder started far from convergence'' predicts a bad answer better than ``the final grid is coarse'' does. It was reverted; the mechanism and the numbers are kept in the source.

That flatness is itself the diagnosis: the refinement ladder is not converging and stopping, it is running to its slab ceiling, because an eV-scale $\Delta m^2_{41}$ over an $11\,000$~km chord demands a slab width below what the ladder will reach. \magnus\ warns on every row.

\smallskip

\textit{Reach: the other way.}---Refined against its own tightest setting, \magnus\ reaches $3.3 \cdot 10^{-10}$ at three flavors where {\tt NuOscProbExact} stalls near $5.6 \cdot 10^{-5}$ at its densest slabbing. The residual between the two codes is $4.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$, the same order as the \emph{looser} of the two curves, so most of it is the closed form's discretization rather than a disagreement about physics. A residual far above \emph{both} curves would have meant a convention mismatch instead, which is the check we make before concluding anything from a cross-code difference.

At $3+1$ the accuracy runs the same way and the diagnosis is sharper. \magnus's residual against the referee is $4.5 \cdot 10^{-8}$, \emph{below} the referee's own discretization error of $4.1 \cdot 10^{-7}$, so the honest statement is that it agrees with the referee to within the referee's uncertainty and this comparison cannot resolve it further. {\tt NuOscProbExact} sits at $2.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$, some six hundred times above that floor. There is no setting of that dial to read off as converged.

The two findings sit together uncomfortably. \magnus\ warns on every $3+1$ row (correctly, since the answer is not backed by a convergence argument) and is also the most accurate answer on the plot. \textbf{A convergence warning is a statement about evidence, not about error}. This is exactly where the two come apart.

The floor is the slab product's. \subsection{The Sun: an observable the others do not offer}
\label{sec:solar_comparison}

Section \ref{sec:solar} showed what \magnus\ returns on a tabulated solar model: forty averaged energies in about $0.7$~s, matching the analytic adiabatic limit to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-5}$. Neither of the other codes has an averaging flag, so the comparison here is between an algorithm designed for the question and an algorithm run harder.

Driven on the same model file, {\tt nuSQuIDS} needs about ten minutes merely to reach a solver tolerance at which its output is a probability at all---and then a further factor of $N$ to average the phase away. At {\tt rel\_error} $=10^{-4}$ it takes $161$~s and returns values from $-1.19$ to $3.09$; at $10^{-5}$, $251$~s and $-0.011$ to $1.007$; only at $10^{-6}$, after $568$~s, does every value lie in $[0,1]$.

One methodological point belongs here, because it is a real trap and it cost us time. \textbf{The obvious guard passes on every one of those rows.} Summing the flavor probabilities and checking they come to one holds to $10^{-16}$ even where the survival probability reaches $2.83$. That is not a bug in the guard: {\tt nuSQuIDS} evolves the density matrix in an SU(3) basis whose identity component is the trace, so the flavor sum is conserved \emph{by construction} however badly the traceless components are integrated. A structural invariant cannot test the thing it is built into. The check that bites is each probability lying in $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Which code to reach for}
\label{sec:which_code}

\tabl{which} answers the question a reader actually arrives with: what to run on the problem in front of them. Each measurement above answers one narrower question. We include {\tt NuOscProbExact} rather than only \magnus\ because the two are close relatives. They take the same arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian, compose the same ordered product of slab operators, and share units, conventions, and parameter defaults deliberately. The choice between them therefore turns on the shape of the profile rather than on the physics in the Hamiltonian.

One line covers most of it. \textbf{If the Hamiltonian is constant over each stretch you hand it, use the closed form; if it varies across that stretch, use the expansion.} An exact algebraic solution beats a truncated series wherever an exact algebraic solution exists. Section \ref{sec:order_is_the_slab_product} shows why the two meet: a piecewise-constant composition \emph{is} the Magnus method at second order, so on a staircase the closed form is doing the same arithmetic in fewer operations. What \magnus\ adds is orders four and six on the same chain, which pays only when there is curvature within a slab for them to resolve.

Two entries in \tabl{which} resist the sorting the rest obey. A supernova shock appears twice with opposite answers; the parameter that separates them is not the physics but the width of the front measured against the oscillation length (Section \ref{sec:shock_comparison}). A tabulated profile belongs to \magnus\ not because tables are special but because the interpolant between samples is smooth, so a method that integrates across a slab has something to integrate.

Because the two packages share conventions, a calculation can be moved from one to the other and run both ways. That is worth doing on any result that matters: agreement between two methods whose failure modes differ is stronger evidence than either one's internal convergence check, which Section \ref{sec:refinement} is careful to describe as evidence rather than proof.

\subsection{Before comparing any two codes' numbers}
\label{sec:conventions_first}

One piece of method decided several results in this section, and would have decided them wrongly had we skipped it.

\textbf{Check that the two codes agree in vacuum first.} If they do not, the disagreement is in the solvers. If they agree in vacuum and disagree in matter, it is in the conventions---a matter-potential factor, an electron fraction, a channel index---and no amount of tolerance will close it. Concretely: \magnus's $V_{\rm CC}$ sits $1.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ below {\tt NuOscProbExact}'s at the same density. Left unmatched, that difference pins \magnus's error on the Earth plane of \figu{prem_plane} at $2.35 \cdot 10^{-4}$ however tight the tolerance, so the curve comes out \emph{flat}---points stacked at one height, above every other code, saying nothing. A trade-off curve that does not respond to its own dial is reporting a convention difference, not a solver. \ref{app:prem_conventions} follows that thread to its end, and finds that even a matched potential leaves a floor. The probability on an Earth chord inherits the relative error of the matter potential essentially one for one. Two codes whose potentials agree to seven digits therefore cannot be compared below the seventh digit of the probability.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have provided \magnus\ \cite{Magnus}, which computes neutrino oscillation probabilities by propagating the evolution operator with the Magnus expansion, for an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, four, or five flavors, through a matter profile that may vary continuously along the trajectory. Neither the expansion nor the commutator-free integrators built on it are ours; what we provide is their implementation for this problem, together with the slab machinery, the adaptive refinement, the exactly unitary exponential, the composition with adiabatic transport, the closed-form decohered limit, and the batching that makes a whole scan one call.

Three properties recommend it, each measured rather than asserted. It is \emph{unitary by construction}: every truncation of the series lies in the Lie algebra, so probabilities are non-negative and their rows sum to one at machine precision at any accuracy setting. The defect does not grow with the baseline, the order, or the tolerance. It is \emph{indifferent to the phase}: a slab is exact for a constant Hamiltonian however many cycles it spans, so what sets the discretization is the profile and not the phase. Measured, the cost grows as $\Phi^{0.21}$ against $\Phi^{0.77}$ for a differential-equation solver asked for the same tolerance on the same problem, and \magnus\ is the more accurate of the two there by nearly three orders of magnitude. It is also \emph{indifferent to the physics}: the Hamiltonian is a callable returning a Hermitian matrix, so five flavors, non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, and a term nobody has written down are the same call.

We are equally clear about where it should not be used. It does not treat open systems: decay, decoherence, and collective effects need a density matrix, which no enlargement of the flavor basis provides. It is not the quickest route to a probability in constant or piecewise-constant matter, where a closed form is better by about $20\times$ on a three-flavor Earth chord and about $400\times$ at $3+1$. Its tolerances are a stopping criterion rather than an error bound (the same caveat that every adaptive solver carries).

None of that touches the case the code was built for: a profile that varies continuously along the path, a Hamiltonian for which no closed form exists, more flavors than a closed form can express, or an observable---the phase-averaged probability of \equ{averaged_varying}---that is better computed than reconstructed. \magnus\ is available from PyPI with {\tt pip install magnuspy}, and from its repository\ \cite{Magnus}.

\section{The expansion terms at any order}
\label{app:terms}
\appresetfloats

Section \ref{sec:expansion} gave the recursion; this appendix gives its structure, which is what makes an implementation to order ten possible without typing $129$ expressions.

Unrolling \equ{recursion}, every term of $\Omega_n$ is a right-nested chain
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:nested}
 \left[\Omega_{m_1}, \left[\Omega_{m_2}, \ldots
 \left[\Omega_{m_j}, \mathbb{A}\right] \ldots \right]\right] \;,
 \quad
 \sum_{i=1}^{j} m_i = n-1 \;,
\end{equation}
carrying the coefficient $B_j/j!$. The terms of the $j$-th group are therefore indexed by the compositions of $n-1$ into $j$ positive parts, of which there are $\binom{n-2}{j-1}$, and since $B_j$ vanishes for odd $j \geq 3$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:term_count}
 \#\,\Omega_n = \sum_{j\,:\,B_j \neq 0} \binom{n-2}{j-1} \;.
\end{equation}
The surviving coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:bernoulli}
 \frac{B_1}{1!} = -\frac{1}{2} , \;
 \frac{B_2}{2!} = \frac{1}{12} , \;
 \frac{B_4}{4!} = -\frac{1}{720} , \;
 \frac{B_6}{6!} = \frac{1}{30240} ,
\end{equation}
and $B_8/8! = -1/1\,209\,600$. Equation (\ref{equ:term_count}) gives $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65, 129$ terms at orders one to ten, doubling from order five onward, which is why the implemented ceiling is a deliberate choice rather than an accident: the terms remain easy to \emph{generate} far beyond it, but the work per slab grows with their number.

The module {\tt magnus.expansionterms} evaluates all of this in exact rational arithmetic at any order, with coefficients returned as fractions so that they can be compared without a tolerance. It shares no code with the numerical core, which is the point: the test suite regenerates the hard-coded group factors and compares them against the derived ones, and separately evaluates the generated terms numerically on a sampled $\mathbb{A}(l)$ and compares them order by order against what the core produces internally. Agreement is at machine precision for every order from one to ten, covering the hand-written low orders and the generated high ones in one check.

Two practical notes close the appendix. Higher order buys a genuinely faster convergence rate in the slab width---measured against a tight reference on a smooth Hamiltonian with a single slab, orders four, six and eight converge as $h^{6.6}$, $h^{8.3}$ and $h^{10.2}$---but the cost per slab grows faster than the order does, and narrowing the slabs at order four or six usually reaches a given accuracy for less total work. \magnus\ warns when an order above six is requested. And, more fundamentally, the series converges only while $\int\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert\,dl < \pi$: beyond that radius no order helps. The answer is narrower slabs.

\section{Structure of the code}
\label{app:structure}
\appresetfloats

\tabl{modules} says what each module of \magnus\ is for. Twelve of them sit under {\tt src/magnus/}, together with the {\tt hamiltonians} subpackage, each carrying a single responsibility that no other module shares.

Two properties of the layout are worth stating because they are easy to break by accident. The Magnus core, {\tt magnus.magnus}, imports nothing from the rest of the package: it is pure numerical linear algebra on an arbitrary matrix function $\mathbb{A}(l)$ and knows nothing about neutrinos, which is why it can be unit tested without touching the probability API at all. {\tt magnus.oscprob} is the only module that imports every other one; it is where physics scenarios, environments, and the numerical core are wired together, deliberately in one place rather than scattered across the modules it draws on.

{\tt magnus.oscprob} is also the largest module, because it exposes an explicitly named function for every combination of flavor count, environment and scenario---about sixty of them. To keep that from becoming sixty copies of the same logic, which is exactly what once let several bugs hide, every one of those functions is a thin call into a much smaller set, in three layers. Layer one is {\tt osc\_prob}, the only function that calls the Magnus core, owning the refinement loop, validation and logging, and itself a first-class public entry point for an arbitrary Hamiltonian. Layer two is four scenario functions---vacuum, standard matter, non-standard interactions, Lorentz-invariance violation---each generic in the flavor count. Layer three is the named wrappers, whose entire job is to repackage named parameters into the dictionaries layer two expects.

The contract between the layers is enforced rather than documented: a wrapper must \emph{not} redeclare any of the fifteen refinement and logging keyword arguments that layers one and two own, and two permanent tests inspect every wrapper's signature and fail if it does. Before that contract existed, every wrapper declared its own copy of those parameters with its own defaults, and fixing a default meant remembering to fix it in sixty places. Inevitably some were missed: a wrapper with a silently different default tolerance than its siblings, a wrapper missing the antineutrino flag entirely, a wrapper whose validation bound disagreed with its siblings.

Extending the package means writing a Hamiltonian, and usually nothing else. {\tt osc\_prob} accepts any callable returning a Hermitian matrix of any size and does not ask where it came from, so a new term is added to whatever an existing builder returns, \ie, to an ordinary array and passed on unchanged. Only genuinely new physics---a term that fits none of vacuum, matter, non-standard interactions or Lorentz violation---calls for a new layer-two function.

\section{The test suite}
\label{app:tests}
\appresetfloats

The repository carries $1207$ tests covering $93\%$ of the library, run on Python $3.10$ through $3.13$ on every push and every pull request, together with a clean-room installation of the built wheel. They are not packaged with the library; from a clone,
\begin{verbatim}
pip install -e ".[test]"
pytest
\end{verbatim}

The suite is not only a regression net. One part of it checks the mathematics against something external to the implementation---the Bernoulli recursion coded independently, closed-form vacuum and constant-density expressions, a tight {\tt DOP853} integration, an exact {\tt expm} where the profile makes it exact. Another checks the properties a probability must have whatever the Hamiltonian: unitarity, the behavior of reversed channels, agreement between the scalar, vectorized and compiled paths. A third asserts \emph{identities} rather than tolerances---the batched scan against the per-point path, a parallel run against a serial one---so that an optimization which changed an answer fails rather than passes quietly. A fourth asserts on the \emph{distribution} of the error over random profiles rather than on individual cases. A fifth guards the layout itself: the documented file tree is generated from a table and checked against the repository, so adding a file fails the suite until the table follows it; the same mechanism keeps this paper's directory in the tree.

\section{A six-code plane, and what it can resolve}
\label{app:prem_conventions}
\appresetfloats

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure*}[t!]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{prem_speed_accuracy.pdf}
 \caption{\label{fig:prem_plane}Deviation from a converged reference against time per probability, for six codes crossing the Earth along a PREM chord at $\cos\theta_z = -0.9$, a baseline of $11\,468$~km, in the survival channel $P_{\nu_\mu \to \nu_\mu}$, at three flavors ({\it left}) and with a sterile state ({\it right}). Section \ref{sec:prem_comparison} compares \magnus\ against {\tt NuOscProbExact} directly, with both codes installed and each refined against its own tightest setting. This appendix does something different and, for a reader choosing between six codes, more useful: it places \magnus\ on a plane of five external codes measured by the companion project, whose numbers are frozen in this repository. \figu{prem_plane} is that plane. It also carries a caution that we think is the single most transferable thing in this paper.

At constant density every code can be handed the same Hamiltonian, so a comparison measures how exactly each evaluates the probability from it. Through the Earth there is no single Hamiltonian to hand over: the density changes continuously along the chord and the Hamiltonian with it; every code---\magnus\ included---must first build its own approximation of the profile. \figu{prem_plane} therefore measures how finely a code resolves the profile rather than the exactness of its probability formula; no exact answer is available to measure against.

The reference is consequently a \emph{converged} PREM solution rather than an exact one: a second-order Richardson extrapolation, $[4P(256) - P(128)]/3$, of a $30$-digit slab product, which cancels the leading discretization error rather than shrinking it. Because that combination is built by cutting the chord the way a slab code cuts it, an error in the cutting would sit in both and cancel from the comparison instead of showing up in it. It is therefore checked against a calculation sharing none of that machinery: an adaptive integration of the flavor state through the \emph{continuous} profile, restarted at every density jump so that no step straddles one. The two agree to $2.3 \cdot 10^{-11}$ at three flavors and $1.2 \cdot 10^{-9}$ at $3+1$, and those values are the dotted floors in \figu{prem_plane}.

\subsection{What limits a cross-code comparison on an Earth chord}
\label{app:the_floor}

The conventions matched before any curve was drawn are not optional. Every code is given the same oscillation parameters, expressed in the convention that code expects; the same PREM implementation, chord, and baseline in eV$^{-1}$; and the same $V_{\rm CC}$. That last one is where the interesting number is.

\magnus's charged-current potential sits $1.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ below {\tt NuOscProbExact}'s at the same density, the two codes rounding the nucleon mass differently. Left unmatched, that difference pins \magnus's error on this plane at $2.35 \cdot 10^{-4}$ however tight the tolerance, so the curve comes out flat---points stacked at one height, above every other code, saying nothing about either. Matching it, by passing an electron fraction of $0.5000948$ rather than $0.5$, is what produces the curve drawn.

It does not produce a curve that falls forever. The reason is worth stating as a general fact about this kind of comparison. \magnus\ is self-converged where its curve flattens---its answers at ${\tt rtol} = 10^{-10}$ and $10^{-12}$ agree to $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$---and the residual against the reference is a smooth, energy-dependent offset rather than a discretization error. It is the potential match running out. Changing the matched scale by a further $1.6$ parts in $10^{7}$ moves the floor from $1.6 \cdot 10^{-7}$ to $3.3 \cdot 10^{-8}$, so \textbf{on this chord the probability inherits the relative error of the matter potential essentially one for one}. Matching two codes' potentials to seven digits therefore leaves a floor in the seventh digit of the probability. No solver setting on either side reaches beneath it.

We do not tune the scale to minimize the residual. The distinction matters. The value used is the one the two codes' own constants imply; fitting it instead would make the figure prettier and the measurement meaningless. What \figu{prem_plane} therefore shows for \magnus\ is an upper bound on its error, floored by the Earth-model agreement, while for {\tt NuOscProbExact}---which shares that Earth model with the reference exactly---it shows discretization error. The two quantities are not comparable below $10^{-7}$, and \tabl{prem} is where the comparable measurement lives.

The transferable version: \textbf{a cross-code comparison through the Earth is limited by the agreement of the two Earth models long before it is limited by either solver}, and any such comparison reaching past $10^{-7}$ without saying how its potentials were matched is reporting a convention.

\subsection{Timing conventions}

Each external code is dialed over the range its own interface offers, each through whatever batched interface it has. One timing convention is worth naming because ignoring it made \magnus\ look like one of the slowest codes on the plane for a reason having nothing to do with its arithmetic. The first call in a session also pays a one-off compilation of the {\tt Numba} kernel, about $0.7$~s; charged to twelve energies, that alone is $58\,000~\mu$s per probability. A user pays it once per session, not once per call. Every timing here therefore discards a warm-up pass and then autoranges the way {\tt timeit} does, repeating inside the timed block until it is long enough to measure and keeping the best of several.
